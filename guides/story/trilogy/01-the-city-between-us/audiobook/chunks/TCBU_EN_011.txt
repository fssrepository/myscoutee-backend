14. Thailand, Cambodia, and the Price of a Ride.

Thailand was not a short stop. I travelled for roughly two and a half to three weeks across different regions: north, west, the eastern Gulf side and the south. Bangkok, Pattaya, Phuket, the route toward the Phi Phi Islands and the crossing into Cambodia each carried a different rhythm.

Pattaya left me comparatively neutral. Other places opened more strongly. In the south, transport often began with a repeated question: did I want a ride? Usually the offer had a price. That was not wrong; it was the local economy making itself explicit.

Once, on the way toward a pier for Phi Phi, I was walking through an unfamiliar area with many dogs. A local person stopped and offered to take me to the pier. This time no price followed. My impression was that he had read the situation before I had and decided that help mattered more than negotiation.

I cannot prove what he thought. I remember the difference in tone.

The Cambodia excursion was compressed almost beyond reason. I arrived around midnight and obtained a visa at the Cambodian entry point with far less preparation than my European bureaucratic instincts expected. I continued into Siem Reap, negotiated with a tuk-tuk driver, visited Angkor and returned to the airport by late afternoon.

What remains clear is the structure: every stage required a new handoff. Border official, driver, ticket, agreed return, another vehicle, another clock. The temples offered scale and silence; the people made the day possible.

15. Moscow, Saint Petersburg, and an Open Gate.

Russia required a different kind of preparation. From Budapest, I needed an invitation for the visa. A travel agency near Oktogon helped arrange it. One office redirected me to another, and another toward the embassy. The absence of an ordinary appointment route made the visa cost several times what I had expected.

Then I arrived and did not know how to buy the bus ticket.

A Russian passenger used a monthly pass to let me through into the passenger area. I had crossed the formal border with documents, fees and repeated instructions. The final small gate opened because an ordinary person saw me standing on the wrong side of it.

Moscow and Saint Petersburg offered opposite scales. Moscow felt radial and monumental: broad approaches, walls, squares and movement arranged around power. Saint Petersburg opened horizontally through water, façades, bridges and the long light of the north. The cities were historically immense. The memory that best explains the journey is still the smallest: somebody tapped a pass.

16. Europe Within Reach.

From Budapest, Europe often began before dawn at a coach pickup point.

Some journeys lasted only a day. Their brevity did not make them trivial. A bus created a temporary group: strangers, couples, a guide, a schedule, a fixed number of seats and a promise to return by night.

In Slovakia, the High Tatras rose beyond forest and weather. I know I visited them. The exact stop is less certain; it may have been Starý Smokovec, Hrebienok, Štrbské Pleso or Tatranská Lomnica. What I remember most clearly is the change in scale when the bus stopped: the timetable remained human-sized while the mountain did not.

Elsewhere in Slovakia, my family roots reached into the Slovak Ore Mountains. Cousins lived there, but they did not speak Hungarian. I met them only once, when I was a small child.

Austria contains several separate journeys in my memory. Vienna includes Schönbrunn—palace geometry, gardens and the Gloriette holding the city at a distance. Another route reached Klagenfurt and Minimundus near Lake Wörthersee, where the world is rebuilt in miniature. It was a strangely appropriate place for somebody who would later attempt to model complicated human systems: whole countries reduced to objects small enough to walk around, their differences preserved through detail.

The Bärenschützklamm was not Vienna and not a palace. Near Mixnitz in Styria, water and rock were crossed through a long construction of bridges and wooden ladders. Progress became physical and cooperative. Every person had a pace; every narrow section required awareness of the person ahead and behind.

Croatia and Slovenia added other one-day memories. I saw Zagreb only in passing through the coach window on the way to the Plitvice Lakes; I did not visit it as a city. Plitvice was the destination. In Slovenia, I visited Bled and Vintgar. I remember them as part of a circular walk. Ljubljana was not part of my journey.
