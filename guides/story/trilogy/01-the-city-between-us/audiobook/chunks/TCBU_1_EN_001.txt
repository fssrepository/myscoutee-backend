A Note to the Reader.

This is a memoir-based account told in the first person. It is also the origin story of a product that is still being tested.

The rooms, journeys, conversations, mistakes and moments of kindness come from my memory. Names have been removed, some sequences have been compressed, and a few uncertain locations remain deliberately unnamed. Where memory and public geography meet, I prefer an honest question mark to invented precision.

All character and scene illustrations in this book are artificial intelligence (AI)-generated visual interpretations. They do not depict the actual participants.

This story compresses decades. Adjacent pages, and especially adjacent seconds in the companion film, do not necessarily describe events from the same year or events that followed one another immediately. Where a year changes the meaning, I state it. Where only the emotional connection matters, I preserve the bridge without pretending that a lifetime happened in one continuous season.

MyScoutee is a built software core, not a claim of proven social outcomes. It does not promise a perfect partner, guaranteed chemistry or complete safety. The idea is simpler and more difficult: help mutual intention travel far enough to become a real, freely chosen meeting.

This first part ends with that invitation. The consequences of the next chapter in my working life belong to Part II.

Prologue — The Cognac Notebook.

The notebook is still on my desk.

Its cover is the colour of cognac held against a window at dusk: brown until the light reaches it, amber where the years have worn the surface smooth. I carried it through rooms that never quite became homes, opened it on trains and buses, and filled it with systems long before I knew which system I was trying to build. Some pages contain numbers. Some contain routes. Others contain sentences that stop halfway across the paper, as if they too were waiting for somebody to answer.

When I look back now, I do not remember my life as a straight line. A village field becomes a railway track. The railway becomes a tram wire over Budapest. The wire becomes the blue-white route of the Victoria line. A wet London window becomes the glass of a bus somewhere between mountains. A pencil mark becomes a network.

Memory edits without asking permission.

Yet one question survives every cut: how can a world crowded with people leave so many of us without a path to one another?

That question did not begin with an app. It began much earlier, with soil on the hands of my family, three disciplines in my mind, a diploma still waiting behind one missing language certificate, a city of extraordinary beauty, and the strange discovery that a person can have a place to sleep without having a place in life.

Part One — Ground.

1. What the Soil Teaches.

My ancestors were small agricultural producers. I do not say this to construct a romantic countryside that never existed. The soil is not sentimental. It is seasonal, physical and exact. Something is planted or it is not. Rain comes or does not come. Work leaves a mark that can be seen.

At eight, I decided that I wanted to become a programmer. The machine that first held my attention was a Commodore 64, but I was drawn to more than the rules beneath its games. I watched their backgrounds: small constructed worlds in which code, colour and atmosphere occupied the same limited screen.

That double attention never left me. Programming rewarded precision. On my journeys, paintings and historic buildings drew me toward forms that could look mathematically exact and still carry the beautiful irregularity of the hands that had made them. I wanted to understand both: the structure that held a thing together and the human trace that kept it from becoming sterile.

That inheritance stayed with me even when my own tools became mathematics and software. I trusted systems that could be understood, work that could be tested, and effort that produced a visible result. I was less prepared for the invisible systems surrounding human acceptance: the rooms offered through friendship, the jobs decided through impressions, the circles that opened only when somebody inside already knew your name.

Like many young people from the countryside, I moved toward Budapest for study and work. It was not because the countryside had no dignity, and it was not because every Hungarian journey must end in the capital. It was my route. The station platform was small; the expectation attached to it was enormous.

Before university, I loved to run. I entered races and learned to think in breath, rhythm and distance. Natural ability helped, but what running taught me was that breathing and endurance usually mattered more.
