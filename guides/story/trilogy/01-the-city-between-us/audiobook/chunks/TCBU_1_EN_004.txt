A third encounter began through an online dating site and happened there. This time the physical space existed, but a ready-made story about me arrived first. I felt immediately stereotyped. The first judgment softened enough for a single night, but not into a shared future.

There was another online introduction before London in which the other woman wanted to continue getting to know me. This time I cannot blame the room, the train or an absent shared context. I became frightened by the possibility of commitment and stepped back. That memory matters because a system did not break every unfinished path. Sometimes I was the person who closed it.

I do not present these women as mistakes or myself as the innocent centre of unfinished stories. Attraction, hesitation, dignity, money, housing, health, timing and expectation were different in every case. We all arrived with problems, and we all risked turning one visible problem into a judgment about the whole person.

A relationship is not the selection of somebody whose life contains no difficulty. It is the slower work of learning what is real and deciding whether two people can accept one another and continue through problems together. That does not mean hiding essential context, abandoning boundaries or enduring harm. It means refusing to confuse a quick label with a complete human being.

In these memories, judgment often arrived before understanding, while important context sometimes arrived too late. Mutual possibility appeared. What we lacked was enough shared truth, time and structure to discover whether we could move forward together.

3. The Key My Parents Gave Me.

Eventually, my parents helped me acquire a flat.

That sentence is short enough to hide the weight inside it. Their help gave me material security at a time when many Hungarian families entered foreign-currency mortgages that later became traps. I did not take that route. I cannot know what would have happened in an alternate life, and I do not claim that every borrower made the same decision or faced the same outcome. I only know that my parents' help placed a solid key in my hand.

The key solved a housing problem. It did not solve belonging.

Budapest remained beautiful outside the window. I had work. I had an education. I had a place that was mine. I still felt that the surrounding human world was conducting a conversation from which I had missed the opening sentence.

This is one of the hardest forms of solitude to explain. Nothing is obviously wrong. The lights are on. The bridges are full. Exhibitions open, musicians play, friends meet friends, and couples find one another in ways that appear effortless from the outside. A person can live among all of it and remain unconnected.

The final work sequence before London made that separation impossible to ignore. Through my previous employer, I spent roughly four or five months working in the United States. Our temporary base was a hotel on the outskirts of Baltimore, and we had a company-rented car. The geography that opened around the work was astonishing. We reached Ocean City on Maryland's Atlantic coast, Virginia, Philadelphia and Washington; travelled between Baltimore and New York by Greyhound; saw Niagara Falls; and visited Amish country. At roughly the Washington–Baltimore latitude, Six Flags lay to the east. Farther inland to the west, we walked what I knew as an Indian Trail along a wooded mountain-top ridge.

In Adams Morgan, I remember women smiling and sometimes making the first approach. I do not turn that into conquest or a claim about American women. What stayed with me was the social lightness of the gesture: for a moment, contact did not feel like a test I had already failed.

The most surprising feeling arrived on the return from New York toward Baltimore. Somewhere on that journey, I felt for the first time that I was going home. “Home” meant a hotel outside Baltimore and a temporary working team, not a permanent address. The feeling showed how quickly repeated routes and familiar people can give borrowed geography an emotional centre.

That American period was therefore not black and white. It was a dream of scale, movement and possibility, but it contained very little private life. Work consumed most of it. In my account, I was driven extremely hard after overtime had been promised. When the assignment ended, I returned to the Hungarian office and continued working there for a time. The promised overtime payment did not follow me home. The hours had already been worked. As the promise grew older, they seemed to be turning into free labour after the fact. I could not make that transformation feel ordinary. Friction grew. I eventually left the employer. I keep the company anonymous because this is my memory of what happened, not an adjudicated claim in this book.
