Yet the house was not a tableau of defeat. People ironed shirts before dates, translated messages, borrowed umbrellas, studied, laughed, failed, tried again and made room for one another because there was literally no other way to move through the hall.

In my rough memory, perhaps one person in three around that household and its circle no longer wanted to return to Hungary. This is not a measured statistic; I would never print it as one. It is the emotional proportion I remember. Some kept family photographs and precise return plans. Some were undecided. Some had formed local ties. Others seemed to have stored the idea of return somewhere they no longer opened.

They could be materially poorer in London and still feel happier there.

That contradiction stayed with me. A home can preserve roots and still contain loneliness. A crowded rented room can be difficult and still contain more human life. Prosperity, shelter, home and belonging are related, but they are not the same thing.

7. The Stranger Who Snored.

One night, I went to sleep sharing the room with one person and woke to the sound of another.

The change announced itself through snoring.

The flatmate who had lost his money through gambling had exchanged his place with an acquaintance. No formal transition reached me. Darkness fell on one arrangement; morning revealed another adult silhouette in the same zone of the room.

There is pain behind that story, and I do not make the debt or the person into a joke. The humour belongs to the housing system: a human life could be replaced overnight while the furniture remained more stable than the residents.

The different snore was a perfect edit. It contained loss, adaptation, embarrassment and survival in one sound.

Years later, when I thought about software that remembers whether people have actually met, I remembered how easily human context can disappear. A person can enter the same physical space without an introduction and become only evidence in the morning.

8. The First Three Years and the Fifth.

The shared bedroom was a bargain I made consciously. The money saved through sharing paid for journeys while the low cost base also allowed savings to accumulate. For a time, the bargain felt temporary and therefore light.

The first three years passed in wonder. Work, museums, colleagues, nightlife, dates and low-cost travel kept opening the world faster than the room could close it.

By the fourth and fifth years, repetition changed the meaning. The same commute, the same rent, the same negotiated space and the same calculation began to look less like a bridge and more like a permanent structure. Alternative housing felt too expensive and unmanageable. That pressure mattered, but it was not the whole reason I returned, and my employer was not the enemy of the story.

What changed was my sense of time.

Temporary hardship can feel almost adventurous when the future remains open. The same hardship becomes heavy when a person realizes that the future has been waiting outside the room for years.

London had not lost its magic. I had begun to ask what the magic was building.

In December 2015, I looked again at a private diagram through which I had been tracking the route toward financial independence. This was not a projection of future independence. The diagram showed that what I had saved was enough for financial independence in Budapest.

The location mattered. Budapest was much less expensive, and the flat my parents had helped provide before London was available to me. It had stood empty throughout the five years I lived in London. In Budapest, the savings and the flat changed the calculation completely. In London, I had never been financially independent.

The London number contained a trap. I had accumulated the savings while living throughout my London years in a shared bedroom. A normal private home in London would consume too much of what I had built, while remaining in the same shared room would turn a temporary bargain into my permanent life.

Housing became my stop-loss. I use that phrase deliberately: the boundary beyond which staying would either spend away the Budapest independence I had reached or require me to keep accepting a life I could no longer inhabit.

That recognition placed me in front of my greatest fear. I had reached the financial target for a viable life in Budapest, but I could not build a viable London home around it. I left London with very little explanation. The company asked whether I wanted to make a complaint. I said no. The real reason had never been the company; it was the question of whether I could direct my own life without waiting for another institution to grant permission.
