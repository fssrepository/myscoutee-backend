In one secondary-school race, classmates caught hold of my arms and tried to pull me back. I still finished first.

I do not turn that finish into a parable about defeating other people. What I remember is the rhythm returning after the interruption: breath finding its place again, and the line still waiting ahead.

People usually read me as quiet and restrained. They did not expect the wilder part. I was never quick-tempered; I might let anger reach my voice once in three or four years. When I finally spoke without softening it, the surprise could silence a room. Reserve was not the same as fear.

I studied economics, programming and mathematics together. In Hungarian, the degree has a name that sounds almost like three professions trying to occupy one line. I liked that. Economics asked what people valued. Mathematics asked what could be proven. Programming asked whether an idea could be made to run.

Years later, those three questions would return together.

2. Budapest, Beautiful and Distant.

Budapest never looked like a city one should need to escape.

The Danube divides it and holds it together. From the Pest side, Buda rises in layers: Castle Hill, stone walls, church towers and slopes that catch the last light. From Buda, Pest opens into boulevards, tram lines and the long ceremonial geometry of Andrássy Avenue. Yellow trams move beside the river as if somebody had drawn a bright line through history. Steam rises from thermal water in winter. Museums, concert halls, markets, cafés and old cinemas offer more lives than one person could inhabit.

The beauty continues beyond the city. The Danube bends through Szentendre, Visegrád and Esztergom. At Visegrád, the river curves below the citadel; at Esztergom, the basilica rises above the water and the border. Gödöllő holds a palace and park close enough for a day. The Buda hills begin before the city has fully ended, with caves, forest paths and the chairlift lifting ordinary afternoons above the roofs.

I knew I came from a beautiful place. That knowledge made the loneliness more confusing, not less.

I finished university in 2004. Because my dissertation had slipped behind its original schedule, the earliest I could have collected the diploma was 2005. Even then I could not collect it: one intermediate-level foreign-language examination was still missing. In reality, I received the diploma considerably later. My first attempt to establish myself in Budapest did not become a settled move. For about a month, relatives let me stay in their large house. There was physical space, and I do not claim that they treated me badly, but I felt that I was imposing on their life. The work that had brought me there did not match what the employer had led me to expect. I felt deceived, and I returned to my parents.

After that, Budapest became a series of one-day attempts. I travelled up for job interviews several times, with the employment centre reimbursing the cost of the journey to the capital. A ticket could carry me to an interview and back, but it could not yet turn the city into a life. Each trip began with possibility and ended with another return to my parents' home.

The Cornfield and Trivandrum.

The professional door finally opened in October 2005, about a year and a half after I had finished university. At that moment I was not sitting in an office or waiting beside a polished reception desk. I was hoeing between rows of corn.

I joined an Indian company that October, and almost immediately the route carried me to South India. I spent about a month and a half in Thiruvananthapuram, also known as Trivandrum, working with an Irish and Hungarian team. The city is Kerala's capital near the far southwestern edge of mainland India: low coastal hills face the Arabian Sea while the higher country of the Western Ghats rises to the east and northeast. It already belonged to an established technology landscape, but the place I encountered could not be reduced to an office or a software park.

The district includes famous visitor beaches. The particular shoreline that stayed in my memory felt very different: I practically saw no tourists there. That is a memory of one beach, not a claim that southern Kerala had no tourism. What mattered was the sudden scale of the change. A man who had just been working a cornfield was now learning how to work inside a South Indian city, with the sea at the edge of it and several national habits meeting inside one team.
