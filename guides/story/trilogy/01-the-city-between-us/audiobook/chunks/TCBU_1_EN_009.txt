Years later, when I designed a path in which one invited person could decline and another could freely accept an available place, I remembered this journey. The point was never to replace friends as if people were spare parts. The point was to admit that willingness and availability are different facts. A plan can fail even when everybody likes the idea. A humane system should preserve the relationship and still allow the event to live.

11. Nearly Fifty Countries, No Collection.

Over the years I reached nearly fifty countries across Europe, Africa, Asia and the Americas.

I made most of those journeys alone, and I organized them myself. That did not mean that I moved without help. I still depended on airlines, hotels, local drivers, hosts, buses, ferries and sometimes an organized excursion. The work was in joining those pieces into one route and accepting responsibility when a handoff failed. The five-person British road circuit was an exception in company, not in authorship: I had planned that route too.

The number is true and almost meaningless by itself.

Countries are not achievements to be collected, and monuments are not evidence that a person understood a place. My strongest memories are often thresholds: somebody explains a route; a driver waits; a passenger makes space; a vendor changes tone; a host points toward what the postcard does not show; a stranger notices danger before I do; a group cooperates because a balloon has landed in the wrong field.

In each new environment, I met a different version of myself. Cairo tested my ability to negotiate. India tested what remained possible when cash suddenly lost its ordinary meaning. Thailand tested my reading of offers and kindness. Russia tested my patience with official routes and my dependence on an ordinary local gesture. Norway tested my willingness to trust a chain of transport I did not control. London had begun the movement, but returning to Budapest did not end it. Some of my largest journeys came later.

The notebook therefore does not preserve a strict timeline. It preserves a principle:

Global route. Local encounter.

The route expands the world. The encounter makes it real. In the difference between the two, I discover something about myself.

12. Egypt: Six Days of Temporary Trust.

I planned six days in Egypt largely by myself. Through WhatsApp, one driver and local hotels, I joined Cairo to Aswan, Abu Simbel and Luxor. The itinerary was short enough to look unreasonable and precise enough to work.

I landed in Cairo around midnight. At the airport, a taxi driver told me that unofficial exchange would be better than the formal counters. His private fare felt too expensive, so I ended up sharing transport with a Sudanese married couple. I was immediately drawn to the woman, and both of them seemed amused by how little I managed to hide it. Then the taxi pulled away and their suitcases, stacked on its roof, fell into the road. Before we had properly left the airport, flirtation had become a practical problem and we already had a story in common.

I reached Giza well before eight, but the site did not open until eight. I waited outside with police carrying steel shields ahead of me and stray dogs wandering behind. When the gates opened, I went in. Before I climbed onto a camel, we agreed a price. When the ride ended, three handlers surrounded me and demanded more. If they had asked that at the beginning, I could have paid or walked away. What unsettled me was that the bargain changed once it was too late to choose.

I paid and walked back, ready to forget it. I had not noticed the posted price guidance and did not know that I had paid above it. A cheerful manager saw my expression and asked what had happened. The photograph of the camel on my phone gave them something to trace. While they searched, I sat between two armed tourism-security officers.

They eventually drove me back in a police vehicle. Asked how much should be returned, I named a figure off the top of my head. By chance, it left the final price close to the posted guidance. I would never have returned for the money on my own. By then, I only wanted the episode to be over.

What stayed with me was that strangers intervened after I had already decided to let it go.

That night, I flew south to Aswan. Its airport lay some distance from the city, and before dawn I was already on the road again. At the edge of Aswan, a stern guard stood high on an armoured vehicle beside a mounted gun. Beyond him, a police car took the lead and our convoy followed it into the desert toward Abu Simbel.

Back in Aswan, my hotel stood beside the Nile. From my room I looked across the water to a beautiful Nubian island. The room felt like a honeymoon suite prepared for two, with only one guest inside.
