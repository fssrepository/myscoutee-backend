After roughly fifty unsuccessful interviews, a job reopened the domestic route almost by accident in August 2019. Then the world closed.

COVID-19 did not make travel impossible forever, and I will not claim that nobody can travel as before. For me, it ended the earlier feeling of carelessness. The open route was no longer innocent. Borders, health, cancellation and uncertainty became visible inside every plan.

The change clarified something that had already been forming. I loved movement, but I did not want to spend my life only passing through. I had learned to enter new countries. I had not learned how to make human continuity survive the journey.

Beauty had given me memories. Local people had made those memories alive. What I still lacked was a way for an encounter to become part of a shared future.

Part Four — Rooms Full of Beginnings.

19. I Came Home to Find a Partner.

I had gone abroad to earn money.

I came home to find a partner.

The sentence sounds cleaner than the life around it. Returning was not a single moment in which the suitcase touched the floor and every intention became clear. Between December 2015 and 10 November 2018, partner-seeking, travel, private creative work and doubt occupied the same suspended interval. Only then did I begin consciously re-entering the country's ordinary working and institutional life. Foreign experience did not automatically create a place for me in the labour market or society. People could read the same years abroad as courage, instability, high expectations, failure, ambition or a sign that I might leave again. I could not know which story another person had already written about me.

Age also became more visible. In my own experience, it had felt less decisive abroad. At home, I increasingly felt that a number could stand in front of the person. I do not claim that age discrimination is uniquely Hungarian or absent elsewhere. It exists across labour markets and inside digital dating. My point is personal: by the time I reached the room, somebody could already believe the most important fact about me was how many years I had lived.

On one of my first public nights out in Budapest after returning, I approached a woman in a nightlife venue. It was Thursday. She looked at me with a grimace, and the expression hit me as culture shock. In places such as Adams Morgan, I had known strangers to smile and sometimes approach first. Now I was back in my own city and could no longer read a room I was supposed to know.

By Saturday, I was organizing Thailand. Within days, I had left. Her expression was one of the reasons I fled into travel so quickly. I was escaping the feeling that Budapest had become foreign to me after I came home.

Still, I tried.

The first rebuff was not the only public-room memory. On another night, I moved toward a woman. Before I could say a word, she exclaimed, "Well, not you!" The sentence closed the space before a conversation began. It remains one person's boundary in one moment, not a verdict on Budapest or Hungarian women.

Another woman was already surrounded by several people. I stepped into the group, asked for a way to contact her and then left her to continue the conversation. We later met. The meeting taught me the opposite lesson from the rejection: I had succeeded in creating a next step, yet once we were together I did not feel the attraction I had expected. Access was not connection, and being chosen was not the same as choosing honestly.

On another evening, a woman and I danced well. I sensed that she was waiting for me to ask for her contact details. Instead, I left the venue with people I knew without saying anything to her. Afterwards they asked why I had not asked. Had I become frightened? The possibility stayed with me because this time no one had rejected me. I had disappeared from an opening myself.

I later found a useful name for part of this confusion: re-entry or reverse culture shock. A person can return expecting home to be familiar, while both the person and the remembered home have changed. I do not use the term as a diagnosis, an excuse or a comparison in which one country wins. It describes the mismatch I felt. My return to Hungary was physical long before it became social, and the missed beginnings contained my own fear as well as other people's boundaries.

I tried because I had not returned to observe my life from behind the window. I wanted a person with whom the journeys, work, ordinary evenings and future could be shared.

20. Two Kinds of Silence.

I joined the apps.

Online dating contains real possibility. People meet, form relationships and build lives through it. My own experience also exposed two opposite failures of attention.

On one side, a person sends interest and waits inside silence. The phone remains still long enough for the stillness to become a verdict.
