On the other side, a person receives so many messages that individual intention loses shape. Attention becomes a crowd. Reading everything requires time, filtering and safety work. Overload can look like abundance from the outside and feel like another form of impossibility from within.

Neither person is morally wrong. The system places scarcity and excess beside each other without building a bridge between them.

A photograph carries enormous weight. Age, appearance and fragments of history arrive before a voice, before a shared task, before we have seen how somebody listens, adapts, jokes, helps or respects a boundary. We form an opinion because the interface asks for one. The opinion can feel complete because no shared context exists to contradict it.

When I returned from London in December 2015, I had just turned thirty-five. For almost two years, I swiped and the phone gave me no match at all. Men crowded one side of the screen; women were fewer on the other. Thirty-five was also an edge in Tinder's age filters. They could look down the age scale toward younger men and still have another face waiting.

At thirty-seven, I changed one field on Tinder. I entered thirty-three instead of thirty-seven and left the photograph exactly as it was. The silence broke. I met women around thirty and some who had not yet reached it. Women older than the age displayed began matching one after another too.

Thirty-three and thirty-seven were four ordinary years once two people sat across a table. On the screen, the number had decided whether the table existed. The face had not changed.

One of those encounters in 2017 made that change tangible. I met a woman whose own picture caught me immediately. That response was entirely subjective; the photograph had told me almost nothing about her.

She worked in financial advisory for a multinational investment bank. During our conversation, she asked why I did not try applying there. I answered indirectly. Money was not the real obstacle. I had still not closed London inside myself. I was living in Hungary, but I was not yet fully here.

From my side, the date felt good. She did not want another. I never learned why. I wondered whether something about me had felt wrong to her, but that was only my attempt to explain an answer I did not have. She did not close the door to continuing the chat, yet a second conversation never happened.

This was still before I officially re-registered in Hungary on 10 November 2018. The encounter did not decide that return on its own, but it nudged me. If I was going to live in Hungary, I could not remain indefinitely outside its ordinary systems while still facing London. I would eventually have to register myself back into Hungarian life and begin looking for work here.

The old world was not automatically kinder. Introductions through friends could exclude, gossip and reproduce closed circles. A person in common could be wrong. Nostalgia is not a product requirement.

What I missed was the useful human function inside an introduction: context before verdict.

21. Rooms Full of Beginnings.

I did not remain behind the phone.

I went to talks, community dinners, language and social evenings, exhibitions, events and other rooms where people had chosen to appear in public. Each room contained beginnings.

A woman I had once dated told me about an unnamed friendship-centred event community in Budapest. After returning from London, I began attending its events occasionally, though never regularly. I had come home alone, and this was one of the places that eased the loneliness. Its identity was broader than dating: it tried to create an open social environment through varied gatherings rather than asking every arrival to carry a romantic purpose. Some formats deliberately mixed strangers so established circles did not close the room. Over time, the community spread beyond one city.

I also went there hoping to find a partner. In the events I attended, people arrived as single adults, yet that shared status did not become an effective path toward a relationship for me. I never knew exactly why. Perhaps the crowd was too large. Perhaps a recurring community made people guard themselves more carefully because nobody wanted an approach or refusal to become group gossip. Perhaps a friendship-centred room made romantic intention feel too exposed. Contact existed, but there was no private reciprocal signal and supported next step that I could recognize and use.

For me, it worked for almost everything else. It gave me somewhere to go so I did not always have to be alone, and I came to know a few people. It offered programs, familiar faces, shared interests, acquaintances and friendship. It did not lead me to the partner I had also gone there hoping to find. That is a personal outcome, not evidence that nobody else formed a relationship there.
