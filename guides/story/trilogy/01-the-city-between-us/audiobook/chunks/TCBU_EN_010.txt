From Aswan, I continued to Luxor with one driver. I chose the road because it allowed us to stop at several special sights along the Nile. I visited Kom Ombo, Edfu and Esna before the road reached Luxor. The journey itself became part of the visit rather than a transfer between two cities.

In Luxor, I stayed outside the usual centre on the west bank of the Nile. In one direction lay the landscape of tombs and desert. Across the water, the lights of Luxor trembled in the river. Serious armed men watched over a tourism economy still recovering from years of fear. Ancient stone, ordinary livelihood and security occupied the same frame.

At dawn, I went up in a hot-air balloon. It did not land where a clean tourism brochure would have chosen. It came down in a vegetable plot. A wall complicated the exit. Some passengers had to pass through a private home while residents, crew and travellers worked together to remove the balloon across the boundary.

For a few minutes, nationality was less important than where to put one's hands.

On my last day, I did not want to exchange more Egyptian pounds. I still wanted a vase for my mother and needed a taxi to the airport, so I told the driver exactly how much money I had left and proposed the arrangement myself: whatever remained after the vase would become his fare.

He accompanied me on foot from shop to shop to help me bargain. The first place had damaged vases, so we continued to a better one. I could not imagine a taxi driver accompanying me like this in Europe, yet there he was, walking beside me and helping to make the same small budget solve two problems. At the second shop, he helped negotiate in a way I, as a tourist, could not have matched. At one point, the driver held his head in his hands. I told the vendor that he was now bargaining at the taxi driver's expense. The vendor laughed and let the negotiation end.

At Luxor airport, I passed through two separate security gates, a degree of layering I had rarely encountered in Europe. From there, the return became a chain of airports: Luxor to Cairo, Cairo to Athens, Athens to Budapest. Two connections carried me home.

During those six days, I also spoke with Syrian refugees. I was compressing a journey I had chosen into the time I could afford; they were trying to continue after events had made movement necessary. We were all away from where we had begun, but not with the same freedom. Everybody escapes differently.

The journey was full of such temporary networks. A hotel, a driver, a manager, a security officer, a resident, a taxi passenger—each carried me across one threshold, then disappeared from the route. The experience was rich precisely because people mattered. It was fragile because the connection had no memory.

13. India in a Cash Discontinuity.

My first stay in India had shown me one southern region through work. It had not shown me the whole country. The Taj Mahal stood more than two thousand kilometres north of Trivandrum even in a straight line, inside a region with different languages, architecture, climate and everyday rhythms. I wanted to see it, so in November 2016 I returned as a tourist for a self-organized five-day journey through India's Golden Triangle.

One day went into flying there through Dubai and one into flying home. For the three days between them, I had arranged a car and driver through WhatsApp to carry me along the Delhi–Agra–Jaipur route.

The journey coincided with a sudden cash discontinuity. On 8 November, the government announced that the existing series of 500- and 1,000-rupee notes would cease to be legal tender from 9 November. It was not the withdrawal of all Indian cash, but it affected the notes on which much ordinary cash use depended. A technical decision at national scale became a human problem in every queue. People waited at cash machines. Others were paid to hold places. Exchange became uncertain. The driver I had arranged through WhatsApp sometimes became the person from whom I had to ask for entrance money.

The arrangement reversed the expected roles. I was the visitor, but I was not financially autonomous. The driver was providing transport and, for moments, liquidity.

Delhi, Agra and Jaipur did not merge into one postcard. Each had its own density, colour, traffic, stone and pace. At the Taj Mahal, another memory separated itself from the architecture. A young woman was travelling with her grandfather. On the bus behind me, I heard a pleasant voice. I turned and, for a moment, lost the ability to arrange language into anything intelligent.

We spoke briefly.

Nothing dramatic happened. Distance remained distance. I did not invent a future that the journey could not support. Still, the memory endured because a voice had turned a famous place into a specific human moment.

That is the difference between reaching a landmark and arriving in one's own life.
