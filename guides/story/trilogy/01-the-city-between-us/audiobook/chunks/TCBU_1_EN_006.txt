When a Polish man colleague was preparing to leave, we began looking for a farewell gift. An Indian woman colleague suggested an artificial penis, something like a vibrator, from a sex shop. It was her idea, and the two of us went into a Soho sex shop to buy it for him. The gift was an affectionate insult: a way of telling him that he was a fucking dick. We liked him, and he understood the joke. What stayed with me was how naturally three people from different countries could share a joke that depended on affection rather than nationality.

Another Indian woman colleague—not the colleague from the farewell-gift memory—once told me that I should write a novel. I worked in that one London workplace through all five years. I did not write the novel then.

A Black colleague also invited me to a wedding. The invitation itself felt like acceptance, but I did not go. I was still an Eastern European immigrant, not the culturally effortless international person I sometimes hoped to appear to be. I feared entering a celebration whose codes I did not know and revealing the culture shock I was trying not to show. Years later, the sharper shock would come not from somebody else's ceremony but from returning to my own country and discovering that home had become the room I could no longer read.

For the first three years, the city felt inexhaustible.

5. The Performance of Not Being Hungry.

My colleagues went out for lunch together, and I went with them. In Soho, lunch was not only food; it was where the workday became human. People loosened, told stories, complained and laughed. Farewell lunches gave the table another purpose: somebody was leaving, so everybody made time to sit together.

It was also expensive.

I was afraid that London would consume my money before I had built any future there. I opened the menu, looked at the prices and performed another version of myself. I sat at the same table and watched my colleagues eat. If somebody noticed, I behaved as if hunger had simply failed to arrive and made it sound like a Hungarian habit rather than a private calculation.

Away from the table, much of my food came down to muesli and two-litre bottles of milk. In the fourth or fifth year, I finally ate with the group once or twice. Their surprise showed how completely the performance had worked.

When the line on my private chart began to look as if it might actually reach the financial independence for which I had come to London, my focus narrowed this far. Every avoided lunch seemed to move it a little closer. The graph could record the saving. It could not record what I watched from the other side of the table.

I do not blame my colleagues. I did not tell them the truth, and they had no reason to know it. That is precisely why the memory matters. Social exclusion does not always look like a door being slammed. Sometimes a person protects himself by presenting the version that costs least to explain. The performance works, and because it works, nobody sees the need underneath it.

By day, I was a competent programmer in Soho. At lunch, I remained visible while the need disappeared.

6. Ten Lives Under One Roof.

At night, the Victoria line carried me north-east to Walthamstow, and the last part of the route continued toward Mansfield Park and the Lee Valley.

The journey appears only briefly in my memory: carriage glass, a reflected face, the dark tunnel interrupted by stations, then the door of a low-rise, flat-roofed shared home beside the canal. At its busiest, as many as ten Hungarian adults lived there, including me.

The flat had roughly four and a half rooms. Inside, space was negotiated by the inch. Outside, the roof opened onto something astonishingly wide: canal water, reed beds, reservoirs, birds and the green reach of the Lee Valley. Mansfield Park rose nearby. Roughly a kilometre away, the Lee Valley Athletics Centre trained athletes for London 2012. A crowded home stood in one of the most beautiful places in which I had ever felt confined.

London outside was radically multicultural. The house inside was a compressed map of Hungary.

We were not identical migrants arranged around a single explanation. One person had left after a family was damaged by a foreign-currency loan. Another came to learn English. Somebody wanted adventure. Somebody was restarting below the level of his qualifications. A night worker returned when others were leaving. A hospitality worker folded an apron beside a private plan. Someone sent money home. Someone had worked for two years and still could not save enough for the return journey he had imagined. Someone lost his money through poker and had to recalculate everything.

One full bathroom served the house, with one separate toilet. I shared a bedroom. Every shelf, mirror and quiet telephone call required negotiation.
