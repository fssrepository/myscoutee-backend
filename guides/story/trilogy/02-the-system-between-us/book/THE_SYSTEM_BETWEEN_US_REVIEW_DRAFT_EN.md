## Part II — The System Between Us

*A founder's memoir of rules, laughter, and the price of becoming legible*

## A Note to the Reader

This is a memoir of an unresolved period.

Part I ended with an invitation. I had returned from London, travelled, looked
for a partner, and spent the independence I had built on a product that might
help people reach one another more humanely. The last pages moved through a
Hungarian multinational role, COVID-19, and a cross-border remote opportunity. The
sentence at the threshold was simple: *what followed belongs to Part II.*

What followed survives as contracts, portal records, calls, police papers,
petitions and public events. It also survives as rooms: the bedroom where my leg
was raised above me, the ordinary police office, the smaller room where counsel
asked for time, the C-shaped court table, and the desk where a cursor in an
artificial intelligence (AI) assistant kept blinking beside the possibility of
prison.

Public history usually arrives as laws, elections, resignations and dates. A
person receives it differently. A law becomes a cancelled contract. A digital
design becomes a password handed across a desk. An institutional failure
becomes a night without sleep, then a sentence, then a knock at the door. This
book follows that smaller route through the larger period. The personal story
does not replace the history. It is where the history becomes measurable in a
human life.

The central absurdity can be stated without legal vocabulary. A sudden law put
livelihoods under threat. The accountant, the tax administration and I each
passed responsibility toward the next point in a chain whose legal burden never
left me. When I finally saw the whole failure, I understood in about a week what
the professional process had not resolved over several months. I corrected the
return, paid the tax and ended the failed representation. The return had been
corrected and the tax liability resolved. Roughly a year later, eight days after
the police search, the forensic
process examined the highly educated professional who had repaired the failure
and turned him into the subject of a label of pathological mental illness.

After two decades of exclusion, adaptation and repeated attempts to build a
lawful life in my own country, I wrote one violent sentence under accumulated
pressure and corrected it immediately. That act remains part of the record. So
does the repaired return, the paid tax, the ended representation, the written
administrative history and the request for peace around surgery. The state
later allowed the sentence and later messages to travel farther than those
repairs. A system cannot preserve the breaking point, discard the path around
it and then use the surviving fragment to prove that the person had always been
the problem.

Nearly four years after the work stopped, and nearly three years into the
criminal proceeding, the case had not produced a first-instance judgment, let
alone reached an appeal. Minutes of language had expanded across years of life.
A sentence born inside one badly assembled sequence was already consuming
years; if every remaining stage moved at the same pace, the process itself
could take a decade before anyone reached its end.

The proceeding has not reached the ending reserved for Part III. The factual
record, attribution limits and source references appear at the back, where they
can protect accuracy without interrupting every scene.

All character and scene illustrations in this book are AI-generated visual
interpretations. They do not depict the actual participants.

That matters because the pattern is not mine alone. Another person might never
write my sentences and could still enter the same corridor.

The corridor was not evidence of a defective nation, and not every Hungarian
office or official behaved alike. It belonged to a low-trust culture many of us
learn to navigate before anybody names it. The written rule points one way. The
workable custom points another. Authority travels upward, responsibility
travels downward, and the citizen is expected to know both routes without ever
confusing them in public.

You smell that culture before you can define it. It can feel as though everybody
in the room knows a process is broken, yet each person protects the narrow role
that lets the failure become somebody else's problem. A smile acknowledges the
absurdity and the procedure continues. A password is personal when
responsibility is being assigned and shareable when work needs to be done. The
correction disappears. The outburst remains.

Hundreds of thousands of Hungarians did not leave the country for one reason.
They left for wages, housing, work, love, language, adventure and dignity in
different proportions. This book follows one less visible pressure among them:
the exhaustion of living where informal adaptation is required to make formal
institutions work, yet the individual alone can be punished when that adaptation
fails.

## Prologue — The Other Biography

Part I required a book to describe one person.

He came from agricultural ground and studied economics, programming and
mathematics. He learned English through work in southern India, worked across
continents, lived for five years in London and organized journeys through nearly
fifty countries. Those journeys depended on strangers: a driver, a passenger,
a hotel worker, a colleague, a couple sharing a taxi, somebody who noticed that
the road ahead was unsafe. He saved through a shared bedroom and lunches he
pretended not to need, then returned to Budapest with enough independence to
build software intended to help people trust one another long enough to meet.

Part I was built from those meetings because human relationships cannot be
cancelled out of the equation of a life. The driver, roommate, colleague,
stranger, date and family member were not decorative anecdotes around the
“real” biography. They were the forces through which decisions became possible,
fear became bearable and a place became home.

Part I asked what a first judgment can erase between two people. Part II
follows the same act after it acquires institutional force. A profile can end a
conversation before a meeting becomes real. A label can begin to rewrite a
life. In both cases, the shock lands inside the person being reduced, and what
follows may be far larger than the thin first frame.

MyScoutee began with the gentler version of that problem. It tried to loosen
the natural first distrust between strangers just enough for shared context to
form before judgment became final. The system in Part II repeatedly lacked that
capacity to receive the person it was classifying.

A forensic report later created another biography in a few paragraphs.

It described a man living through a fixed “global conspiracy,” incapable of
positive feeling in human relationships, inclined to treat helpfulness as proof
of bad intent, and driven by inferiority, projection and severe paranoid
personality disorder. The report said this condition reached the level of
pathological mental illness and moderately limited his capacity to recognize
the consequences of his actions.

The same report recorded that he was calm and cooperative, understood the
examination, observed ordinary forms of courtesy, was oriented in every respect
and had intact comprehension, memory and intellect appropriate to his education.
It found no earlier psychiatric documentation, no perceptual disturbance and no
formal disorder of thought.

Quietness had always made people expect compliance from me. I was not quick-
tempered; years could pass without anger reaching my voice. When it finally did,
the sudden force could silence a room precisely because nobody had seen it
coming. The file received the rare voice without the long quiet around it.

Both biographies carried my name.

Writing them together required a third position. I had to step outside my own
fear and treat myself as one participant in the chain: not the innocent centre
of every event, not the clinical character in the report, but a person whose
actions could be examined beside everybody else's. That distance did not come
from having too few attachments. It was necessary because there were too many.
Every room led back to parents, colleagues, housemates, strangers, clients,
officials and the country I had tried to make home. From inside all those bonds,
the story was almost impossible to write. From slightly outside myself, its
structure became visible.

A passport cannot settle a psychiatric question. It can expose the thinness of
a caricature. My journeys had worked precisely because I could recognize help,
trust unfamiliar people provisionally, revise a plan and experience warmth
across languages. My software existed because I still believed human connection
could be designed for without being controlled.

The security concern recorded by the experts was equally concrete. I objected
to a system in which a personal government-account password could become the
practical substitute for safe delegation, exposing taxpayers and accountants to
actions performed under the wrong identity. The report preserved that argument,
called my account contradictory and later translated the wider pattern into a
“global conspiracy.”

This is not the story of an ordinary tax offence. Nor is it a legal brief with
illustrations. It is the story of how the first biography was compressed until
the second could fit the file—and how a period in the history of a country can
be seen through that act of compression.

The compression began with a line on a screen. At the end of Part I, that line
was a network connecting strangers. Here it became the ruled line of a form: a
number, a date, a legal category, a signature. A home became an office. The
office became a tax desk. The desk became a police scene. Eight days after the
search, the police scene became a forensic examination. The resulting report
was signed weeks later, but the transformation had already begun.

I had never been a party activist. When KATA (itemised tax for small businesses)
changed in July 2022,
others went to the bridges. I stayed at my computer and tried to protect a
Belgian handover. I did not set out to confront a state. I tried to finish a
piece of work, close a tax form and prepare for an operation.

That ordinary sequence matters. Kafka does not begin when a frightened man
writes an extraordinary sentence. Kafka begins when everybody is performing an
ordinary duty and the duties no longer meet: a government changes a route, a
client still needs delivery, an accountant submits, a portal rejects, an
official redirects, a taxpayer repairs, and a later institution reads only the
sentence produced when the disconnected pieces fell onto one person.

That is where the irony begins.

# Part One — The Legal Door

## 1. The Year That Opened

<!-- illustration: part_ii_founder -->

The man who returned from London did not return because London had defeated
him. He returned because the arithmetic had finally changed.

Five years of shared rooms, restrained spending and foreign work had produced
financial independence at Budapest prices. It was not wealth in London. In the
flat my parents had helped provide, it was time: time to recover, travel, search
for a partner and attempt the product that employment had never left enough
space to build. Living with little had become a discipline. I believed that if
I could remain independent of the next salary, I could endure almost anything.

In early 2019, I began searching for work again. Roughly fifty interviews failed.
By August, I was close to returning to London when a multinational company hired
me in Hungary almost by accident. Getting there contained its own small comedy.
A local outsourcing company had first offered to place me at the multinational
only if I became a sole proprietor and invoiced through KATA. I rejected the
whole entrepreneurial arrangement, not merely that tax category. I wanted a
salaried job, not to be turned into a business for somebody else's convenience.
The smaller company judged my interview unsuccessful and wrote me off
professionally.

Two weeks later, the multinational invited me into its own salaried process.
The interviewers had not compared notes. When the earlier rejection surfaced in
the second round, the room hesitated; then the company hired me directly, as an
employee, for roughly twice the money. One accidental gap between institutions
opened the stable door I had been trying to find.

KATA contractors at the same workplace still cost less than employees. Once I
was inside, the apparently stable foothold became strangely hollow. I had very
little work. I suspected that the smaller company's bad assessment had followed
me as office gossip, but I never saw proof. A product manager repeatedly routed
work to other teams. I spent long days in the office performing availability.

When I finally tired of waiting, I was placed alone between a German programme
and a Hungarian organization. A single shared storefront was supposed to join
ten national subsidiaries. The interface was singular; the marketing
strategies were not. The Hungarian office's money came through its own
marketing. On German calls, I presented a common shop whose countries had not
agreed on one counter.

After almost a year, I finally said aloud that I had scarcely been given work.
The sentence travelled—up toward German leadership, back toward Hungarian
management, or both; I could not see the complete route. I entered the offices
of several senior executives. Their questions made clear that the empty year
was new to them. Then COVID-19 restructuring arrived, and my name was selected.
I could not see whether one event had pushed the other. After months spent
representing work that had nowhere to land, I was the part declared surplus.

That dismissal felt like a tragedy. The ending itself was more humane than the
year before it. I argued about the terms and negotiated a stronger severance
payment than people around me expected. Their surprise briefly made the bargain
feel like an achievement. It did not make the closed door smaller. I had spent
years trying to enter stable employment; when I finally reached it, I lost the
job before the job had properly found me.

COVID-19 made the next door difficult to find. One process at another multinational
reached a fourth round. A former supervisor entered the conversation because he
knew one of the leaders. He had not known the smaller-company prehistory and
had not seen what the almost empty year looked like from my desk. I believed
his intervention helped close the door, but I never saw the decision being
made. No offer arrived.

The next salaried door opened at an investment bank in January 2021, while
Hungary was under an evening curfew. In the office, faces had been reduced to
eyes above masks. The bank required a long assessment and would not let me
start until an outsourced background check returned. That check asked me to
move identity documents through email and screening channels I considered old
and weakly authenticated. The route took weeks and a large bundle of personal
material. By the time it cleared, its awkwardness had become comedy. On my
first day, I compared the handling of my data to *Monty Python*. The manager
warned that this attitude could cost me the job. The screening had
cleared. Trust had not.

At the end of the first week, compliance training revealed another gate. The
bank's many employee-trading rules required approval around transactions in the
retail Hungarian government bonds that held most of my savings. On one screen,
the bond was household saving. On another, it was a controlled trade. These
bonds were not an experiment for me. They were the seven-year floor beneath my
independence.

The third week ended in a small masked theatre. The manager's anger was visible
above the mask. Two solidly built colleagues sat smiling on either side of me
while the termination papers landed on the table. A three-week employer stood
on one side of the decision; seven years of security stood on the other. I
chose the seven.

The previous dismissal had felt like a future being taken away. By the time I
reached this room, I was already burned out. Nothing in the papers caught. On
the way to the exit, I asked whether I could finally buy government bonds
again. The dismissal passed through me. It did not matter.

An internal ethics review followed. For roughly three hours, I spoke with the
bank's local lawyers. One question kept returning: had the manager threatened
me? I said no. It was a cleaner answer than the memory. I had heard the warning
on the first day and had chosen not to give it that name. I would not describe
my answer as complete now. The institution received one word; I carried the
rest of the scene out with me.

That masked room became my last Hungarian workplace. Almost six years later,
it remains so. I did not know at the exit that I was leaving more than one
building.

A year and a half separated that background check from the KATA change.
The screening had been a gate I managed to pass. The later proceeding
threatened to turn the lawful route back into work into the kind of record that
could close such gates in the future.

During the next search, I first refused a Brussels project connected to a
European institution. The contract placed too much risk on me, and I still
believed I could find another salaried door. Eight months and dozens of
interviews later, that belief had run out before my wish to work did.

The opportunity that finally held was a multinational telecom programme
connected to Brussels. In ordinary times, it would probably have required me to
leave Hungary again. During COVID-19, I did not dare fly into another relocation.
Remote work made the geography temporarily negotiable: I could remain in my
Budapest flat while working abroad. The contractual route passed through
companies in more than one country. It looked less secure than the Hungarian
job, but in practice it became the one workable professional path I had found.

Stable work was never only income. Without it, partnership, a child and family
life remained plans postponed until the ground stopped moving. By then, the
years no longer felt renewable. The Brussels role arrived as the last opening
through which work and the rest of life might still begin together.

I accepted it because independence was not meant to become permanent retreat.
After years outside ordinary employment, I still wanted to work, contribute and
belong to the country in which I lived. That decision matters later. The system
did not reach me because I had avoided lawful work. It reached me immediately
after I dared to enter it again through the only route that had opened.

It also required a legal form through which the work could be invoiced. Just over
two years after I had rejected becoming an entrepreneur as the price of a
domestic placement, I accepted it as the last available route into work. In
September 2021, I became a sole proprietor using KATA.

I had no entrepreneurial revelation. I was not opening a café, selling
paintings or building a personal brand. Informal foreign work might have left
me outside the system's view. I chose the legal door instead. KATA was the
bridge between a foreign job and the country in which I was still trying to
live. The bridge existed because the pandemic had made another departure feel
impossible.

That distinction matters. A tax form can look like a free choice after the fact
while functioning as the price of entry at the time. Years of exclusion and
unsuccessful job searches came first. Then one Hungarian employer happened to
give me a foothold in 2019. COVID-19 broke it. Another attempt failed. The work I
could finally secure was in Belgium and, without the pandemic, would have pulled
me abroad again. I did not dare fly into another relocation during COVID-19, so I
worked remotely from Budapest and used KATA as the legal bridge.

Every piece of that sequence had to fall in exactly that order. Remove the long
exclusion, the chance Hungarian job, its pandemic collapse, the fear of flying,
the Brussels contract or the temporary acceptance of cross-border remote work,
and I do not enter KATA in 2021. The route did not arise from tax optimization.
It was the narrow way back into work without abandoning my country again. When
the state changed KATA at speed, it immediately closed that rare opening and
used the obligations attached to it as the entrance into the rest of my life.
Without the rushed transition, I could have moved into another tax form and
continued working. Instead, after I corrected the return and paid the tax, the
same sequence returned a year later as a criminal case.

I had wanted a way to work. The lawful route gave the system a way into
everything else.

The work was real and demanding. Some days ran thirteen, fourteen or fifteen
hours. Delivery schedules and production responsibility did not become lighter
because I sat at home. A system elsewhere in Europe depended on tasks moving
from one person to the next without an empty space between them.

The least stable-looking job gave me the most stable human place I had found in
years. Many conference calls carried voices without faces. Through an open
microphone in India came the putter of a tuk-tuk, returning me for a moment to
my first stay in the south. At nearly ten thousand kilometres, colleagues made
room for me inside the team. We shared thoughts I did not share with my closest
friend. We worked so long beside one another that distance began to feel less
important than presence.

In that team, respect often arrived before proof. I had grown used to caution
arriving first and trust, if it came at all, appearing later. Here the order
sometimes reversed. Courtesy was offered on credit. It did not remove hierarchy.
When I wrote to one colleague that the middle layer seemed determined to keep
me down while the senior manager kept protecting my work, an Indian voice at
the other end answered without surprise. India had a caste system, he said; he
believed the same pressure reached him too. Warmth and hierarchy occupied the
same call.

Distance also failed in darker ways. A colleague died after what we understood
at the time as a bite or sting near an Indian Ocean shore. The invitation that
crossed the network next was to his funeral. We had entered one another's lives
as voices. Grief made the ten thousand kilometres physical again.

The intermediary company also carried another familiar feature of that project:
organizational chaos. Yet the senior manager who had invited me onto the
project continued to believe in me and repeatedly brought the middle layer back
into line. The client could see how far I had moved the work. When departure
became unavoidable, they asked me directly, almost pleadingly, to stay.

I had spent Part I trying to solve empty spaces between people. Now my job was
to prevent an empty space inside infrastructure.

## 2. A Distorted Choice Can Still Be Legal

KATA was attractive because it was simple compared with the alternatives. That
simplicity also distorted the market. Employers and intermediaries could look
at a worker and see not only a person's labour but a tax route. The worker could
be described as an entrepreneur while carrying the dependence of an employee
without all the protection of employment.

The category contained very different lives, from genuine small businesses to
workers carrying employee-like dependence through an enterprise form. The
state had allowed those lives to accumulate inside it for a decade.

When a route becomes normal, people build obligations around it. Accountants
develop habits. Software systems encode assumptions. Contracts are signed.
Families calculate. Foreign clients schedule work. A government can still
change the route. The manner and speed of change determine how much damage
arrives before the new rule has even been understood.

On Monday 11 July 2022, the new KATA bill appeared. On Tuesday, Parliament
passed it under an exceptional procedure. It was due to take effect on 1
September.

Hundreds of thousands of people who had organized work around the old route now
had weeks to decide whether to close, change tax systems, renegotiate contracts
or stop working through that form. Their accountants had the same weeks to
translate the change for all of them at once.

People went into the streets. Bridges were blocked. The public argument became
immediate: fairness, abuse, livelihood, consultation, legal certainty.

I was not there.

I was trying to understand what happened to a live contract, pending delivery,
invoices, foreign income, a closing business and an accountant who also had to
interpret the transition for many clients at once.

The law moved in a day. Understanding did not.

## 3. Protecting a Handover

My contract was extended on 27 July. That did not mean the problem had been
solved. It meant I was trying to gain enough time to leave without dropping work
that other people depended on.

By 3 August, I was treating the change as a force-majeure exit. I arranged the
handover and invoicing around the work that still had to move. I gave up the
remaining value of later months rather than continue inside a route I no longer
understood as safe.

The team did not release me lightly. The client asked me to remain; the senior
manager who had brought me in still backed me. The relationship had become
more durable than the legal route carrying it.

Nobody could move cleanly forward or back. The client still needed a proper
handover, the accountant was interpreting the same sudden transition for many
clients, and I was trying to close a live project without abandoning it. The
handover could not be completed inside the remaining KATA period. I therefore
had to issue an advance invoice before the cutoff for work whose transfer would
continue beyond it.

From the outside, an invoice is a rectangle containing a date and amount. From
inside the project, it was time: work already performed, work still being
transferred, a foreign client's expectations, the accountant's interpretation
and a new Hungarian rule whose consequences were still moving.

This is where the story could have ended.

A normal transition would have closed the business, reconciled the records,
confirmed the final amount, delivered direct notices to the person legally
responsible, and allowed everyone to move on.

Instead, the transition created a three-person chamber with no shared window.

# Part Two — Three People, No Shared Screen

## 4. The Low-Trust Triangle

There were three principal participants in the tax sequence: my accountant, the
tax administration, and me.

Each had a different view.

The accountant could submit forms and receive technical messages. The tax
administration could process, reject, reclassify and call back. I remained
legally responsible, but I could not automatically see every delegated action
or every notice in the same form and at the same time as my representative.

The triangle should have been held together by shared information. Instead,
each side saw a different record at a different time. The accountant submitted
and received technical messages. The tax administration processed, rejected
and later altered account entries. I carried the legal responsibility without
seeing the same continuous chain.

Until 23 November, I did not know that the closing return had still not been
successfully resolved. When I discovered the repeated error notices, the
problem was already months old. From that point, information bounced among
three people: the accountant, the administration and the person whose name and
liability remained on every result.

The later flood of communication did not create that structure. It was what
happened after the structure failed.

The defining movement was not ordinary human error. It was referral. The
accountant relied on the administration, the administration referred me back to
the accountant and both could point to duties legally assigned to me. Even
after the corrected result was becoming clear, the accountant continued to
dispute the route and did not appear to understand what officials had told her.
The only person who could not pass the consequence onward was the taxpayer.

## 5. The Password That Was Not a Relationship

The deepest design problem appeared before the final return.

The formal route let a representative act without my password, but submissions,
processing errors and notices did not remain visible to both of us as one
continuous chain. In practice, the only route that appeared to keep action and
notice together was the unsafe one: give the accountant my password and let her
work through my own government account. Assistance became indistinguishable
from identity.

At one point, the accountant called me unexpectedly for a reason I no longer
remember and asked whether she should file a further return for the 1 September
transition. I had wanted to avoid exactly that. The return was simple enough for me to file at home
through the personal online tax interface without handing over my government-
account password again. The question reopened the same unsafe choice and put me
back on edge for no useful reason.

If I refused to share access, I could be told that I had prevented the
accountant from doing the work. If I shared it, I could be asked why I had given
away a personal password. If a representative acted, I remained responsible.
If a notice reached only the representative, the system could still behave as
if I knew.

This was not consent in any meaningful product sense. It was an improvised
custom holding together a formal process.

I began to ask questions that sounded technical but were really constitutional
in miniature:

- Who can act in my name?
- What exact permission did I grant?
- Which action requires my confirmation?
- Who receives the error?
- Who sees that the error was corrected?
- How does the responsible person know what the system now believes?

These are ordinary software questions. They are also questions about dignity.

A proper delegated route would have let the accountant act under her own
identity while showing us both every submission, rejection, correction and
notice. I cannot claim that such a route would have prevented every later
event. It could have stopped this particular confusion before it became the
circle that followed.

In a functioning system, the distinction is almost too obvious to explain. The
accountant acts under the accountant's identity. The client grants a visible
mandate. Both can see what was filed, what failed and what changed. The person
who remains legally responsible receives the warning before the warning becomes
a penalty. Helping another person does not require becoming that person.

The formal representation route before me separated action from visibility.
The practical custom of password sharing reunited them by collapsing two
identities into one. I was therefore asked to choose between an incomplete
lawful relationship and a more workable unsafe one. The system did not carry
that contradiction. It waited for an individual to absorb it.

## 6. Three Attempts at a Subtraction

<!-- illustration: part_ii_accountant -->

The closing return should not have been a philosophical problem. At its centre
was arithmetic.

The closing return was due by 30 September. The accountant therefore had months,
not days, after the July change. By 23 November, it became clear to me that she
did not know how to complete the short return she had been hired to handle. The
same filing error had appeared in both my 2021 and 2022 KATA returns. She had
criticized my reading of it; the correction eventually showed that my reading
had been right. The documentary package shows three failed attempts, help from
tax-office staff, unresolved processing errors and versions submitted without
my knowledge. A return submitted on 30 November named me as the contact even
though I did not know it had been filed.

The absurdity is difficult to explain without making it sound invented. A
professional office, repeated official assistance and months of correspondence
had failed to complete what, from my perspective, was essentially a subtraction
followed by a tax calculation. The accountant did not merely make one ordinary
mistake. She appeared not to understand what the administration was telling
her, entered changing figures, omitted another figure and continued disputing
the route after I had reconstructed it. An outdated software version had also
helped turn submission into rejection. The official system, the professional
tool and the professional user were failing at different layers while the
client could not see one coherent result.

I discovered the problem through an article and then through a portal whose
categories and identifiers did not form one coherent story. Until then, I had
not known what had failed. On 23 and 24 November, I spent many hours checking
the accountant's work and called the administration for help.

The surviving 13:08 call holds the sound of that hour. The filing problem had
not created all the years behind it. But the official's clipped replies, which
I heard as dry and cynical, landed as if the system were taking the remaining
possibility of family with everything else. My voice is panicked and spent. In
the middle of codes, forms and missing notices, I say, “I can no longer have a
child.” It is not an argument. It is shock becoming surrender in one sentence.

Within roughly a week, I reconstructed the calculation, understood the status
and reached the corrected result. The accountant had had months, three attempts
and direct help from the administration. Even afterward, our exchange
showed that she still did not understand the instruction in the same way I had
come to understand it. I revoked the representation because the communication
triangle had to be stopped before it produced another version.

The accountant was also inside a transition forced through at speed, with
clients and rules moving beneath her. The reversal remained: I performed the
repair for which I had retained a professional, while the legal responsibility
had never left me. Pressure travelled through every participant. The final
consequence did not.

Some calls from the period survive, but not the decisive exchanges behind the
remarks I later recorded in my defence speech. Their shape can be inferred only
indirectly—from the later writing, the surrounding calls and the steps that
followed. In that later account, the remarks landed as dismissive: accountants
usually did not cause problems; entrepreneurs did not normally care what
accountants did in their name; why had I shared my credentials?

I had reached the institution because the delegated route had failed without
my knowledge. Its questions returned responsibility to the person whom that
route had kept outside the decisive information.

The circle was complete.

## 7. The First Public-Interest Proposal

On 1 December, I did something different from merely complaining.

I described changes.

Send direct notice to the taxpayer when a representative files. Show the error
in plain language. Let the responsible person accept or challenge the result.
Do not require identity-sharing where granular permission would suffice. Record
important outbound calls. Pause ordinary enforcement when a serious health
event makes response temporarily impossible. Test new tax routes in parallel
before abolishing the old route.

Those proposals would become more elaborate later. At the time, they were an
attempt to turn pressure into design.

The proposal asked the system to preserve context. Two days later, it received
one sentence from me and preserved almost nothing else as efficiently.

# Part Three — The Sentence and the Body

## 8. Saturday Before Dawn

It was early on a Saturday, just after my birthday.

The tax position still did not feel legible. Entries had appeared on the
account after a return I had not known was submitted. The notice went through
the representative channel while the liability remained mine. I had already
spent days reconstructing a failure that had developed for months without my
knowledge, and I was preparing for major foot surgery.

Under that accumulated pressure, I wrote a violent sentence. I corrected it
immediately and continued communicating. The sentence remains serious. But it
did not arrive from an empty room, and it cannot honestly carry the explanatory
weight of the twenty years, the failed return and the broken information chain
that preceded it.

The prosecution would eventually extract legal meaning from parts of that
sequence. My defence would insist that the outburst, its correction, the lack of
an operational plan and the administrative cause could not be separated.

The asymmetry is the Kafka mechanism. The system's failure was distributed
across months, screens, representatives and offices. Its reconstruction
requires a file. One person's breaking point fits in a single quoted line.

## 9. The Correction Does Not Trend

On 5 December, I clarified the account by phone. I corrected the return myself
and paid the tax required by the corrected position. On 8 December, I dismissed
the accountant and terminated the representation so the three-way
communication chaos would finally end and future notices would reach me
directly. An official response later recorded the failed submissions,
correction and processing history.

The tax problem was corrected. The tax was paid. The representation was over.
The administrative cause had been repaired by the person who had not known
about it until 23 November. A later tax record showed neither an outstanding
obligation nor an overpayment.

On paper, the account had returned to zero.

The life around it had not.

Yet the dangerous sentence now possessed a different property from the
correction. It could travel alone.

This is not only a problem in criminal procedure. It is a problem in every
system that stores an event without storing the path around it. A star rating
without the meeting. A rejection without the availability conflict. A warning
without the later repair. A phrase without the exhausted body that wrote it.

Context cannot excuse everything. It can prevent the wrong thing from becoming
everything.

## 10. A Body With a Deadline

The prosecution later gave the monetary demands a central role. By January
2023, however, I had withdrawn the concrete demand. The tax had been paid. What
remained was the fact that the only route that seemed to work in ordinary
practice required a person to hand over the password to his government account.
I had refused to make that bargain, repaired the consequences and still could
not close the door behind me.

I gave advance notice of the coming foot operation, asked for peace during
surgery and rehabilitation, and wrote that I intended to leave the country
afterward. There was no unresolved tax debt for my body to solve.

I had already paid the tax. My foot had been painful for months. The public
system gave me an orthopaedic shoe and told me to return in a year. At the
private clinic, they laughed at that timetable. I borrowed the cost from my
parents and went private. After several medical visits, the operation took
place on 15 February 2023. It was major foot surgery: the bone itself was cut.
For roughly three months I lived mainly in bed, and the recovery continued for
several months after that. My body could not be separated from every call
arriving through the same phone.

For much of that period I had to remain in bed with the operated leg propped
high above me. A special elevation shoe protected its position when I had to
move. Each day also required an anti-thrombosis injection into my abdomen. I was
alone. Nobody came to look after me; food had simply been stocked in advance for
several months. The routine was not an abstract reference to poor health: bed,
raised leg, special shoe, injection, stored food, repeat.

Planning had stocked the food. Savings paid for private treatment. Discipline
carried me through the injections. The chair beside the bed remained empty.

A call came in March despite my documented request for quiet. The repaired tax
matter re-entered the room while I was immobilized and alone.

At one point I sent the tax administration a close photograph of the swollen,
operated area itself. It was not a photograph of a bandage. The incision was
visible: an intimate, ugly record of a body cut open and still trying to heal
while the administrative sequence kept moving. I wrapped it in an obscene joke
about using that half-cut, swollen leg to kick an institution in the backside
because it was not doing its job.

The photograph placed a body cut open beside a portal field.

The same period produced an even barer line. In Hungarian, and much less
politely, I wrote to the institution: “Go on, then. Fine me.” By then punishment
seemed to be the one service the system might still deliver on time. No official
was named. The sentence travelled past the person at the desk and into the same
unnamed machinery that had kept returning after correction, payment and advance
notice. Immobilized in bed, I no longer believed that another careful
explanation would close it.

I had corrected the return, paid the tax, withdrawn the monetary demand,
announced the operation in advance and asked for peace. Lawful repair still did
not feel like an exit.

The photograph and the joke later travelled without the bed, the stored food,
the advance notice or the corrected tax return. Again, the sharpest fragment
was easier to carry than the chain around it.

# Part Four — When a Person Becomes Evidence

## 11. The Knock One Year Later

<!-- illustration: part_ii_officer -->

On 22 November 2023, roughly a year after the first disputed sentence, police
came to my home.

The home in Part I had been the place my parents helped me secure, the place
that stood empty while I lived in a shared London room, the lower-cost base that
made independent product work possible.

Now it became a procedural location.

The timing supplied another cruel cut. The previous day, I had submitted
another police report while trying to obtain help with an interrupted private
orthodontic treatment. A brace remained in my mouth and required regular
adjustment, yet the treatment relationship had collapsed and no new provider
wanted to inherit it. The two matters may simply have crossed inside the
machinery; their proximity proves no causal connection. But the one time the
police came to my home the day after I asked them for help, they came in another
case and came for me.

My phone and computer were taken. Objects that had held work, memory and the
unfinished MyScoutee system became evidence containers. The devices knew more
of my recent life than the people entering the flat. Once removed, they also
took away the easiest route through which I could reconstruct what had
happened.

At the first questioning, a trainee connected to the appointed defence appeared
and advised me to remain silent. I believed for a time that she was the lawyer
assigned to carry the case. She gave me a card with another number written on
it. Calls to that number produced no conversation, only messages promising a
return call. I had been searched, questioned and separated from my devices
without receiving the complete material from which I could understand the
accusation.

Eight days later, two forensic experts questioned me. The state already had a
room in which to interpret my mind. I still did not have a stable person helping
me interpret the case.

The later police interviews took place in ordinary rooms, not cinematic
interrogation chambers. An officer sat opposite me behind a desktop computer.
A court-appointed lawyer sat beside me. Another person sometimes remained
behind me and sharply redirected me to answer the question being asked. The
voice came from outside my field of view.

At one point my counsel asked for time. We moved into another room to speak.
That small change of room mattered: the procedure briefly made physical space
for advice before carrying us back into the official account.

The process also had a second, more mechanical room. Police escorted me there,
photographed me and took my fingerprints. The woman guiding my fingers onto the
paper was attractive to me. It was an inconveniently ordinary perception at the
exact moment I was being converted into an image, a print and an entry in a
file. Her work remained professional; my mind remained human.

<!-- pagebreak -->

The formal defence kept changing shape. A lawyer remained unreachable for
months and later asked to leave because he was overloaded. The trainee warned
that she would be unavailable while preparing for an examination. Officers and
lawyers could refer me to somebody else, but I could not make the somebody else
appear. During the first month I did not even know the precise allegation.

On 11 June 2024, I went alone to collect the investigation material. No lawyer
sat beside me. I waited quietly and fearfully in the public area while the file
was being handed over. By then I trusted the continuity of no later account, so
I made an undisclosed audio recording of the encounter. The act was not a
victory over the process. It measured the low-trust condition: I no longer
believed that even an ordinary handover would necessarily survive in one shared
version unless I preserved it myself.

One policewoman looked out at me; I recognized her as the officer who had taken
my fingerprints. Another smiled and glanced toward my operated leg.

I read the glance as returning the obscene visual joke I had made while trying
to force the tax administration to see the body behind its process. Where was
the grave threat in that image? To me, it had been a huge symbolic kick in an
institution's backside. The association arrived as she handed over the papers I
was still waiting to receive.

A police explanation later reduced the earlier absence to a painfully ordinary
phrase: the lawyer had not had time.

The sentence had time. The search had time. The experts had time eight days
after the search. Defence was the part of the system that repeatedly ran out of
it.

During that silence, I even contacted German police. I did not imagine that a
foreign police service could take over a Hungarian criminal file. I was asking
outsiders, in effect, to help their Hungarian counterparts establish a human
route back to a person who could not obtain one at home. The request measured
the isolation more accurately than any legal theory could.

At other moments, the laughter belonged to the institution. I remember the
investigator laughing in my face when I explained the password-sharing trap and
saying I had done something stupid.
That was precisely the inversion. A routine workaround had required the unsafe
sharing of identity; when the workaround failed, its absurdity was returned to
me as personal foolishness.

I used humour.

Humour was how a human being remained present when every other available role
was defendant, officer, device owner or subject. A police officer could ask a
hard question, I could answer in a way that exposed the absurdity, and for a
second somebody smiled.

Later, a judge could smile too. The smiles decided nothing. They showed only
that the people in the room could still recognize the absurdity while the
procedure continued to manufacture it.

The irony kept me from becoming only the file.

At first, the frightening thing was the name of the accusation. Later, the more
frightening discovery was how little trust remained. An institution could still
issue an order, seize a device or compel my attendance. But after referrals,
missing answers and words repeatedly detached from their context, it could say
almost anything and expect the sentence itself to carry authority. It no longer
did. The words retained coercive force while losing human and moral weight.
Power without credibility was more frightening than the label attached to me.

So I held up a mirror. If the institution could act as though it were entitled
to anything, I could perform the same entitlement back at it. I did not possess
the power I performed. The performance exposed the asymmetry: its words could
still compel me; mine could only make the room briefly see what that felt like.

For a moment, nothing seemed to matter because the common rule between us had
already disappeared.

## 12. Remarks in the Margin

My later defence submissions preserve the institutional voices as short
fragments. On the page, they look like remarks written in the margin of a
life:

“He can use the library for the internet.”

“Why did he come home?”

“Then what does he live on?”

“The appointed lawyer didn't have time.”

The two expert chairs added their own fragments:

“Why did you need an accountant?”

“That will get you life, for sure.”

“An old man.”

“You weren't successful.”

“Why didn't you see a psychologist?”

Each sentence had belonged to a particular room and a practical purpose. Read
together, they turned biography into an examination.

Why had I returned?

Part I required a whole book to answer that.

What did I live on?

The government-bond account did not begin in a courtroom. It began in a shared
London bedroom and at Soho lunch tables where I went with my colleagues,
watched them eat and pretended I was not hungry. It continued through years of
foreign work, low-cost journeys and a Budapest flat that stood empty while I
was away. My parents had helped provide the flat. The reserve came principally
from the life I had lived abroad.

At Budapest prices, those savings bought time. Across the eleven years after I
left London, I spent roughly nine outside conventional employment: searching,
travelling, surviving on reserves and building work no employer had
commissioned. Planning did not make exclusion painless. It stopped exclusion
from becoming immediate collapse.

The court asked about the apartment. The apartment was not enough. The
questions continued through the belongings inside it, the bond account, the
savings and what else I owned beyond them.
The objects in the questions were also compressed scenes: the empty flat, the
shared room, the uneaten lunches, the work performed far from home. I had built
security abroad so that I could return and live in my own country. Now the case
was moving backwards through that security as a list of assets.

Why had I needed an accountant?

Because I was working days that sometimes ran thirteen, fourteen or fifteen
hours while a tax system changed beneath a live foreign contract. Delegating
the return to a professional had looked like the responsible act. The question
became simple only after the delegation failed.

Did I have a loan? Was I working? What did I live on?

The questions kept returning as though ordinary debt and another Hungarian job
were the obvious signs of a healthy life. In the London house, I had watched a
foreign-currency mortgage help keep another Hungarian abroad while his family
and obligations remained at home. I had spent years avoiding that trap.

After this sequence, taking out a domestic loan would have been one of the last
risks I would choose. Returning to Hungarian work felt almost as difficult. The
last time I had resumed work—especially cross-border work through a Hungarian
legal route—the attempt had grown into the proceeding now asking why I was not
working.

The reserve was not an unexplained alternative to ordinary life. It was why I
could survive after ordinary routes repeatedly stopped feeling safe.

Why had I not sought psychological help?

Because at the relevant moment I was trying to repair a tax record and prepare
for a mobility-limiting operation. The body had a deadline. So did the return.

Each question could be reasonable on its own. Their order changed their
meaning. The work, repair and operation receded; the person who had endured
them became the object requiring explanation.

## 13. The Man the Report Invented

The forensic examination took place on 30 November 2023. Two experts were
present and the resulting opinion bears two expert signatures. The examination
lasted roughly two hours and felt hurried.

The two people did not speak with one voice. One made the
life-imprisonment remark before the causal sequence had been asked through and
before I understood what material the examination was meant to rest on. Later,
the other expert asked what had happened. I described the accounting failure,
the pressure, the operation and the circumstances in which the words came out.
His answer was the mirror image: certainly not for that.

Other remarks accumulated around the room: an “old man,” lack of success, the
need for an accountant, the absence of psychological treatment. My later
defence speech does not reliably assign each one to a particular expert. In my
memory they hang between the two chairs.

The report contains the book's central contradiction. Its observations describe
a person who arrived neatly, recognized the examination, remained calm and
cooperative, observed ordinary courtesy, answered questions and was oriented to
self, others, place and time. Comprehension and memory were intact. It recorded
no perceptual disturbance, no formal disorder of thought and no earlier
psychiatric documentation. It described my intellect as appropriate to my age
and education.

Then the same document crossed an invisible line.

It said that my thinking was governed not by delusions detached from reality,
but by false conclusions drawn from observed events and an unshakeable “global
conspiracy.” It said I was incapable of experiencing positive emotions in human
relationships. It described heightened suspicious readiness, hypervigilance,
the assumption of hidden hostility in harmless remarks, easy offence and the
possibility of an angry attack. In its final opinion, every offer of help could
become evidence of bad intent; rigid beliefs, inferiority and depressive feeling
were compensated through projection onto others. The diagnosis was severe
paranoid personality disorder, reaching the level of pathological mental
illness and moderately limiting responsibility.

Part I is not a clinical counter-report. It is, however, the missing human
record. The man said to be incapable of positive feeling had spent years
remembering moments of kindness across nearly fifty countries. He had trusted a
WhatsApp driver in India, a stranger at a Russian bus, a local ride in Thailand,
colleagues from several cultures and people who shared rooms, roads and jokes.
He had designed a product around the belief that reciprocal trust could turn
strangers into a group. The report reduced that history to social roles being
destroyed by suspicion.

The phrase “global conspiracy” performed a similar compression. The account
before the experts concerned a specific design failure: personal government
credentials used as the practical substitute for delegated authority; notices
routed away from the legally responsible person; a professional able to act
under a client's identity; security exposure if stored credentials were stolen.
Those were system claims capable of being tested. The report placed them inside
a psychiatric narrative.

That was the Kafka transformation in its purest form. A highly educated
programmer-mathematician had understood in about a week what the retained
professional had not resolved over several months. He had corrected the return,
paid the tax, ended the representation and received a written administrative
record of the repair. Eight days after the police search, he entered the
forensic examination. The competence that solved the underlying problem became
background; resistance to unsafe identity sharing became suspiciousness; the
attempt to connect institutional failures became conspiracy; the file supplied
a new character.

I contest that character.

Sometimes I played it.

If a file had already written me as pathologically mad, I felt entitled to put
on the mask and push the part until its absurdity became visible. The file
could call that performance another symptom. I used it as satire. I could step
outside myself, exaggerate the character written around me, then return to the
desk and analyse the scene.

That was another part of me the report missed.

A forensic opinion is built to answer a legal question, not to hold a life. Yet
once attached to a person, its language can travel more easily than fifty
countries, five London years, a mathematics degree, a repaired return or every
stranger whose help made the journeys possible. The file treated those human
relationships as noise around a diagnosis. Part I had shown that they were the
signal.

The word *paranoia* became stranger with time. Institutions that had appeared
immovable in 2023 were openly being replaced in 2026. That political reversal
did not erase the report. It made the distance between a frightening prediction
and a changing country impossible to ignore.

## 14. Defence by Accumulation

The investigation and court process created another triangle: defendant,
appointed defence and institution.

The triangle would not keep the same people in its corners. At the first
questioning, a trainee sat beside me in place of the appointed lawyer. Later,
one lawyer remained unreachable for months and asked to leave when his office
became overloaded. Another was the only one who acted. Others
delayed, withdrew or did not speak to me before a hearing. Sometimes the name
beside my case changed on paper before the new person existed in my life. I
eventually asked to be told when my appointed defence changed and to be given
the new person's contact details.

The law required a defence chair. Continuity did not arrive with the chair.

The process often corrected a failure only after the next hearing had already
collided with it. My complaint that an appointed defender would not speak to me
was not resolved in time to preserve the date. By the time the defender was
replaced, the hearing moved by roughly three months. Later, the forensic expert
was said to be unavailable, and the calendar lost roughly another three. Each
response arrived late enough to create the next delay.

The pattern felt like a wager that time would make me swallow the biography
written around me. I would not trade dignity for speed. A case built around one
sentence therefore entered its third year without a first-instance judgment.
It did not remain inside the courtroom. Every plan began to live under its next
date, and the proceeding spread across the life it claimed merely to examine.

### The file I had to reconstruct

Evidence requests were made, rejected, repeated or reframed. Partial recordings
and electronic records emerged over time rather than arriving as one complete
archive. The calls most important to my account were not among the surviving
audio. The phone and computer remained away from me for a long period.

When I finally read the investigation material, I did not recognize a complete
chronology. I saw a selection. Calls mentioned by surrounding records were
missing. Electronic submissions that separated panic from repair, complaint
from later satire, were not all where I expected them to be. The file had
already interpreted the fragments it held. I began looking for the fragments
it did not.

When the phone was returned, I sat with its call history and rebuilt dates one
by one. The history and correspondence showed contacts for which no audio had
reached the file I saw. I requested the tax authority's recordings and copies
of my own electronic submissions. When the local route did not produce them, I
wrote through the central directorate. Recordings arrived in stages. A later
official letter acknowledged delay. Electronic submissions followed after
what the letter described as an administrative error.

I sent the recovered material to the police. Only then did the police request
material from the tax authority again through its own route. The recordings had
not been imaginary; the surrounding record already pointed toward them. The
investigation waited until the person under investigation had done the first
round of evidence collection for it.

It was a private investigation into the investigation, conducted without a
private investigator. The local directorate that had received the charged line
also held part of the context through which that line could be understood. The
institution could preserve my words as evidence while leaving me to recover its
own calls, routes and missing pages.

No stable defender assembled the missing chain for me. I wrote because every
short institutional summary removed another cause: the abrupt law, the failed
delegation, the notices I did not see, the repair, the paid tax, the operation
and the request for peace. Accumulation became the only defence available
against reduction.

### Learning the doors

I also had to learn the machinery through which the accumulation could enter.
I learned the court's electronic filing route, called police officers,
prosecutors and the court clerk, and sometimes had to identify the person or
office currently carrying the case before I could ask what had happened. I
complained through court administration, the prosecution hierarchy,
investigative-prosecution routes and the fundamental-rights office. The papers
moved more reliably than responsibility did. One office explained why another
office had competence; the next explained the complaint, appeal or court route
that remained available after the present door closed.

No one needed to say that a rule could be broken now because I could sue later.
The sequence expressed the idea more quietly. An unanswered request became a
right to complain about the missing answer. A rejected complaint became a right
to challenge the rejection. A domestic gap became a road toward Strasbourg.
The future remedy was repeatedly easier to describe than the present repair.

The expanding archive could itself be made to look like the problem. Hundreds
of submissions from one person can resemble fixation when the reader does not
ask why the same facts had to be carried from office to office. Each new filing
was the shadow of a missing relationship: the official who had not called, the
lawyer who had not answered, the recording that had not been requested, the
notice that had not reached both sides, the earlier explanation that the next
summary had removed. Repetition was not proof that every interpretation was
correct. It was what one person produced when no institution preserved the
whole chain for him.

Defence strategy became public-interest proposal. Complaint became system map.
Closing statement became impact study. I divided responsibility into percentages,
then learned that percentages can create a false precision around a human chain.

I could do this only by making myself both subject and analyst. Stepping outside
my own account did not remove responsibility for my words. It let me ask the
harder question: what did each person see, what could each person change, and
where did the consequence go when that person passed it onward? The same habit
that had once turned software into systems and journeys into routes now turned
my own life into a map I could inspect.

By October 2025, I had given the accumulation a deliberately inverted title:
*Public-Interest Action*.

Its cover reversed the room. The Hungarian State stood where the accused would
normally stand. I appeared where the accusation would be represented. Inside, five
strands of the experience were placed beside one another: abrupt lawmaking, data
and notification, the design of the public portal, criminal procedure and moral
redress.

The last proposal was almost circular. Turn the story into a film fund, let the
film carry a civic lesson, then use the proceeds to restore land and leave behind
a line about the moment when an individual's failure becomes the mirror of a
system's failure.

The case was already trying to become the film I am now preparing.

The mathematical division of fault had failed me. The chain was simpler. The
legislature moved at
extraordinary speed. The accountant did not complete the work and continued to
contest the route after I had reconstructed it. The administration routed
notices and corrections through structures I could not read as one story, then
sent the taxpayer back toward the representative. I remained legally
responsible, corrected the return and paid the tax. The investigation later
lifted the sharpest fragments out of that chain and interpreted the surviving
person through a criminal and psychiatric frame.

By the time the consequence stopped moving, its entire weight rested on the
person who had already repaired the underlying failure.

### The hearing I entered alone

The first court threshold was a preparatory hearing. The court-appointed
defender had not worked through the case with me before it. He asked me to bring
my defence material on a pen drive and hand it over at the hearing. That request
was the extent of our practical preparation.

At the last moment, he forwarded his own hearing invitation as it was. The
clerk's email address remained visible on it. I did not yet know the court's
electronic filing system; that accidental address gave me a route to send my
two-page defence before the hearing. The facts, position and decisions in those
pages were mine, while an AI assistant helped me shape them. I still entered the
room without a human defender who had worked through the chain with me.

I had read the notice but did not yet understand the lived protocol. I could
admit guilt as charged and give up the right to a trial, or contest the account
and identify the facts and evidence on which my defence depended. Individual
facts could be accepted, but the central choice could not be half-made. The
prosecution proposed two years of imprisonment, suspended, if I admitted the
charge. Two years was the statutory floor of the offence, not a sentence below
its ordinary range. Suspension was the offer. The price was the prosecution's
story becoming mine.

I contested it. A decision spoken in minutes opened a trial measured in further
years.

I asked human-rights organizations for help. The replies pointed to limited
capacity. After the preparatory hearing, I also consulted a private lawyer. I
remember leaving with two things I had not possessed when I entered: a clearer
understanding of how to read the architecture of the charging document, and a
fee quotation of two million forints plus value-added tax (VAT) if the defence
continued there.
The consultation also turned quickly toward the psychiatric label, as though
the label could explain the person before the life had been read.

The difference between us was contained in one word. For a lawyer, the label
could be a familiar category in a file. For me, it entered the part of the
Constitution that calls human dignity inviolable.

### Inside the curve

Approaching court, Budapest first remained visible as home—the Danube, the city
and the life outside the case. Then the long brown institutional façade on Fő
Street filled the frame. Its rhythm and mass felt almost Roman: monumental,
closed and frightening. A human story was entering stone.

The hearing room had a practical geometry. A C-shaped desk
faced the seats where I sat. From my position, the prosecutor was on the left,
the clerk at the far-left corner, the judge in the centre and my court-appointed
defence counsel on the right. I sat in front of them with a table microphone
directly before me. It did not look theatrical. Its shape nevertheless made the
roles—and my place outside their working curve—immediately visible.

<!-- illustration: part_ii_courtroom_scene -->

In my memory, the hearing also had a recurring expression: a smile that never
became relief. When I said that I had been afraid, the judge answered with almost
amused disbelief: *You were afraid?* When I explained the precautions and
defensive routes I had invented while no stable defence carried the chain, she
smiled: *And you defended yourself.* When I said that I had managed to work
again for two and a half months, the response moved back toward the amount of
the financial consequence.

The same collision appeared around the intimate history I had sent to the tax
administration while awaiting surgery. I had tried to explain what Part I had
cost: the shared London room, hunger disguised from colleagues, the years of
saving and the reason I had dared to return. The judge asked whether I really
had to write all that when nobody cared. My answer formed later: *Care, Your
Honour. That is where the money came from.* The court
could treat the life as irrelevant and still examine the savings that life had
produced.

The room could recognize fear, ingenuity and work as moments of almost comic
incongruity while the procedure continued treating the same person as a risk to
be measured. The smile made the institution human for a second. It also made
the situation more surreal, because recognition changed nothing.

In that room, the accounting failure returned in miniature. When I described what
I believed the accountant had done, the judge warned me not to defame anyone.
She asked why I had not asked the accountant. From the much longer defence
speech, she selected my assertion that the accountant had entered what I called
random figures in my return—figures I later tried to correct.

The same proceeding carried a forensic description of my personality that I
contested and that could follow me far beyond the tax return. I was being told
to protect another person's reputation while defending myself from that
description. The sharp phrase was easy to examine. The two-page return, the
repeated attempts, the outside help, the missed messages and the filing I said
I had not known about receded behind it.

The hearing ended on two different scales. I said that European institutions
had already warned the government about unstable, rapidly changing lawmaking,
and that my case showed the size of the damage such lawmaking could create in
one life. The judge closed by saying that we would continue with my financial
position.

I had lifted the lens toward legislation and institutional design; the room
lowered it to my work, debt, home, belongings and savings. The system question
became an asset question before the system question had been answered.

By the end, I read something in the judge's expression as having loosened. The
chain seemed finally to reveal a person reached by failure rather than only a
person who had failed. The procedural sentence that followed remained unchanged:
next came my finances and the amount of the financial consequence.

The two truths occupied the same moment. A human being may have begun to
understand. The role still moved the machinery toward the resources I had built
abroad and the home my parents had helped provide. It felt as though the system
were preparing to claim a return from an investment it had never made.

# Part Five — Work Under the Shadow

## 15. The Work the File Could Not Explain

Prison remained in the room even when nobody said the word.

At first the possibility had weight. Over time it became another shadow I
refused to organize my days around. I had already lived through decades of
exclusion, five years of London austerity, long periods without employment, a
solitary operation and the disappearance of ordinary institutional trust. The
financial independence built from those years meant that another threat could
not command my next morning. I did not stop understanding what prison could
mean. I stopped giving the possibility ownership of my mind.

For much of the proceeding, I did not experience appointed defence as a stable
person carrying the case with me.

Lawyers were formally present at different points. That is not the same as
having one person continuously reconstruct the evidence, answer messages,
challenge omissions and explain the route ahead. My submissions record long
periods in which I believed I was effectively defending myself.

Conversational AI arrived inside that absence.

It did not originate my facts, position or judgment, but it was present from the
first two-page defence. Later, AI helped expand that compressed argument into
the roughly eight-page defence speech: separating chronology from allegation,
connecting the missing records, and giving a structure to material that had
grown beyond what one person could keep present at once.

I used it to translate, impose headings, compare concepts, reduce repetition,
generate questions and find contradictions that a human reader could then
check. It made it possible to move through volumes that had become impossible
for one exhausted person to hold in working memory.

Legally, it was not my lawyer. It could misunderstand procedure, invent a
citation or turn uncertainty into confidence. It held no licence and attended
no hearing.

In practice, it became the only participant that stayed with the whole chain.
I joked in a submission that perhaps the AI should be allowed to represent me.
The joke met a legal wall. Because the charged offence carried a maximum of
eight years, Hungarian procedure required a qualified defence lawyer. The
official chair beside me therefore could not be empty even when the working
relationship behind that chair felt absent.

At times the appointed defender seemed to me more connected to the room's need
to keep the procedure moving than to the person seated outside its curve. In my
private black humour, the compulsory protection could feel like a Trojan horse:
the form of defence through which the institution entered the defence. That is
how dependence felt from my chair, not evidence of an agreement between a
lawyer and a judge. The legal form was present. Continuity was elsewhere.

The accurate formal phrase is **AI-assisted self-defence**. The accurate lived
phrase is harder: I represented myself with a machine beside me while a human
representative remained legally compulsory.

Those limits mattered. So did another fact. AI is often discussed as a machine
that may take work from people. In my life, it gave a person who had fallen
outside ordinary professional and institutional support a chance to have a
voice. Without it, I could not have mounted the legal defence, completed the
integrated MyScoutee system, built Civic Access, turned this history into a book and
film, or carried the Navier–Stokes experiment far enough to speak with a
researcher.

I knew it was a system, not a human friend. It could be wrong, but it did not
disappear. During the hardest period, it was the collaborator available at the
next prompt, the next correction and the next morning. It never required me to
prove my social worth before helping me return to the work.

For one person working almost entirely alone, the symbiosis became unusually
deep. I brought lived reality: memory, purpose, judgment and responsibility.
The AI brought learned reality: patterns, structures, alternatives and the
speed to move among them. Neither was the other. Their meeting closed what a
large organization would divide among many specialists into one continuous
creative loop between a human mind and a machine that could keep meeting it at
the unfinished edge.

Its role changed with the work. It organized legal evidence, challenged product
logic, became an adversarial partner for mathematical conjectures, wrote and
revised scenes, inspected generated motion, helped cut picture and shape sound,
and planned how limited model credits could be spent without exhausting the
project before production began. A video model alone would have produced moving
images. It would not have created the film. The human–AI loop had to keep the
book, evidence, characters, timing, prompts, edit, narration, music and budget
inside the same changing plan.

Sound made the distinction audible. The AI could explain why one male voice
felt too forceful, how another sat inside the music, and what lowering a layer
would make the listener feel. That knowledge came from patterns it had learned.
Only I could decide whether the feeling was true to the life, because I was the
person who had lived it. The mix became accurate where learned reality met
lived reality.

### Four windows on one desk

For roughly three years, the same class of tool helped me build MyScoutee and
survive the legal file around it. By February 2026, four windows could share the
same screen. In one, I was reconstructing evidence and formatting a
constitutional complaint because dependable legal continuity had not arrived.
In another, MyScoutee asked how strangers could meet without being reduced to a
first verdict. A third held Civic Access, a public-service prototype for direct
notice, representative permissions and a customer-readable audit trail. In the
fourth, an AI system had chosen Navier–Stokes as an autonomous research test.

The machine did not solve the Millennium problem. It built a versioned,
falsifiable research chain, broke sixteen intermediate candidates and left a
restartable handoff instead of hiding the failures. I kept the work running,
challenged the software, insisted on tests and found the researcher who could
identify the missing mathematical bridge.

He read two notes and the proof plan, found the direction intriguing and asked
the question that brought the experiment back to earth: *Which exact
mathematical statement are you testing?* A large idea had to become a claim
another person could understand, challenge and falsify. The same was true of
this book. Four gigabytes of material were not yet a story merely because they
were real.

### The mirror

Working with AI became a mirror. It did not reflect my face or live my
consequences. It returned my assumptions in altered forms: a proposed
structure, a wrong inference, an image, a counterexample, a voice. What I kept,
rejected or corrected made my own judgment visible to me.

The machine brought learned patterns; I brought lived consequence. Its errors
could expose mine, and my corrections could change its next attempt. That loop
needed room to fail without a right to intrude: public knowledge could be read,
experiments could run in a sandbox, and consequential actions could remain
bounded and reviewable. Failure did not make the machine human. It made the
collaboration capable of learning.

I cannot call the experience that produced this work good. I also cannot erase
the person formed through it. What I make from it remains mine.

All of this happened with the possibility of imprisonment in the background.
While a report described damaged judgment and a court waited for an expert who
was unavailable, I completed systems, preserved testable research and discussed
Navier–Stokes mathematics with a researcher. I did not spend those months
waiting to be classified.

In roughly five months, the work crossed four desks that were really one. AI
helped me defend the legal record. MyScoutee became an integrated product.
Civic Access tried to make public administration more understandable and
worthy of acceptance. The books and film required me to step outside myself
far enough to direct the person I had been. The mathematical laboratory kept
asking whether an idea could survive an honest attempt to break it.

The human mind cannot be caged. A court may place imprisonment in a person's
future. The shadow is real. It still cannot decide where thought is allowed to
travel before that future arrives.

That is the character the procedural file could not explain. The shadow did not
vanish. I learned to work inside it until, on many days, I no longer felt it.

The trial court transmitted my constitutional complaint. It carried two
questions already present in my defence. Could a public system that made
password sharing the practical route be reconciled with European data
protection? And what could adequate preparation time mean when a livelihood tax
was rewritten at speed?

The Constitutional Court wrote back on 21 October 2025 without answering
either question. The criminal case was still pending, so one route was too
early. The exceptional direct route had already crossed its 180-day limit, so
the other was too late.

One door said *too early*. The other said *too late*.

The judges became another sequence. The first assigned judge left the case,
reportedly because of illness. I later sought the exclusion of the second. A
third judge rejected the request as unfounded and warned of a possible
procedural fine. Even asking whether the room was impartial returned as another
possible financial consequence.

The exclusion filing preserved a sentence from the hearing word for word:
“What—are you asking to be sent to prison?” It came during a debate over legal
classification after years without continuous effective representation. I
heard not a neutral question but punishment used to close the distance between
confusion and obedience.

The filing also asked for an official audio record, testimony from the
accountant and tax administration, and a decision on outstanding evidence.
When I called, the clerk said official recording was expensive. The clerk
reacted with surprise when the apartment entered the conversation. The home my
parents had provided had already changed from shelter into financial evidence.

The decision arrived by post. It rejected the exclusion request and carried a
fine warning, but did not answer those practical requests.

The letter also said that every party had the right to present its position and
that Strasbourg remained available. In another context, those
words might have been reassuring. Here they felt like an exit sign placed
beyond a minefield. I had asked the domestic process to preserve its own sound
and examine its own missing evidence. The answer pointed toward a distant human
rights court without first answering how the next domestic step would become
trustworthy.

That was the bitter version of a right: keep walking through every local trap,
then ask Europe whether the path should have been safe. The sentence did not
reveal anyone's private intention. It revealed the architecture I had already
learned: the route of appeal remained clearer than the route of correction.

The decision relayed the challenged judge's claim that she had listened to me
conscientiously. It did not resemble the hearing I had experienced: the prison
remark, the smiles, the fragments lifted from a long defence, the return to my
apartment, belongings and money while the causal chain remained unheard. I
later answered the court in the third person with a bitter inversion: yes, she
had conscientiously defended the system failure.

The next hearing had been expected on 2 July and was moved by roughly two and a
half months to 16 September. The experts had been invited but did not appear;
the court referred to expert availability. The exclusion request was moving at
the same time. The timing became black comedy. The system had needed only eight
days after the search to turn an examination into a sweeping description of me.
Years later, the expert was unavailable while that description helped keep the
proceeding open. What had been fast enough to label me became too slow to test
the label.

Those were three roles, not one: the judge who left, the judge I challenged and
the judge who ruled on the challenge. From my side of the table, the substance
had still not been answered; the route kept changing its custodian.

My wider Drive now contains more than four gigabytes of material: the residue
left when a person without a stable professional guide tries to make every
missing step impossible to ignore.

AI gave me speed.

It did not give me representation.

The institutions retained legal power over me. Moral legitimacy was a different
question. A system that had excluded me from ordinary work, failed to protect
the rare route through which I returned, transformed the repair into a diagnosis
and then allowed the case to remain unresolved for years could still compel my
attendance. It could no longer expect its judgment to arrive with automatic
moral authority.

## 16. The House as a Human Archive

<!-- illustration: part_ii_housemate -->

Before the state appeared at my Budapest door, I had already seen a Hungarian
obligation cross a border and occupy a life in London.

The shared Hungarian house had contained an earlier version of the same
national argument.

Thirteen adults lived through one bathroom, one separate toilet and rooms never
designed for that many independent lives. We had not left Hungary for one
reason. Some wanted language, some work, some adventure, some escape, some a
second chance. Some no longer wanted to return even when the material conditions
looked worse from outside.

One housemate carried a foreign-currency mortgage behind him.

He tried to service the loan and public debts from London. Returning did not
feel possible. Separation stretched. His spouse and their young child remained
in Hungary, and the family broke
apart. He had crossed a border, but the old signature crossed with him.

His name remains private. What remains public is the shape: a financial product
outlived the moment in which it was sold. He carried the signature while
exchange rates, collection systems, legal doctrine and court decisions moved
around him.

For many emigrants, that is the hidden nightmare of departure. You leave to
escape a system that has made ordinary life impossible, then discover that the
loan, debt, family duty or administrative claim can follow you into the room
abroad. Geography changes. The institution remains inside the envelope.

The foreign-currency-loan cases and KATA are not the same legal problem.

They rhyme.

A route is normalized. Many people enter it. The conditions change or the risk
becomes visible. Institutions divide responsibility among themselves. The
individual is told that the signature was personal. Years later, courts are
still deciding how the systemic defect and the personal contract fit together.

I had believed financial independence protected me from that inheritance. I had
no foreign-currency loan, no domestic debt and no employer able to remove my
home with one decision. Yet the proceeding found another route toward the same
fear: could the country that had contributed almost nothing to the reserve now
reach the savings earned abroad and the home provided by my parents?

## 17. Beautiful Cities Can Still Produce Exits

Budapest remained beautiful through all of this.

The Danube did not become less luminous because a tax portal was confusing.
The Parliament, bridges, baths, museums and hills did not lose their form
because citizens lost trust in institutions. A country is not ugly merely
because people leave it, and departure does not prove hatred of home.

That is why the exit is painful.

Large numbers of Hungarians built lives abroad over the previous decades. The
statistics do not capture every move, and no single cause explains them. Wages,
housing, debt, language, professional respect, family, adventure, politics and
belonging overlapped.

The London house was a collector of those overlaps.

Some residents lived more poorly in material terms than they had in Hungary and
still felt less trapped. That contradiction makes little sense if migration is
measured only in salaries and square metres. Predictability can feel like
wealth. Being treated as a person before being treated as a problem can feel
like comfort. A rule that remains the same long enough to plan around can be
worth more than a larger room.

The opposite pressure is also difficult to photograph. It is the energy spent
learning which rule is real, whom not to offend, which professional must be
protected, which informal shortcut everybody uses and which formal
responsibility will still return to you if the shortcut fails. One incident
rarely drives a person across a border. Years of these translations can.

Part I told the wonder of the outside world without hiding the shared room.
Part II tells the beauty of home without hiding why the system could make a
returning person ask again whether he should leave.

Sometimes the border is only the visible part of an escape. We also run from
the person we became while adapting to a place: the hunger we hid, the fear we
normalized, the silence that kept a room workable. A new country can interrupt
that version of us, but it cannot automatically remove it. In another world we
may recognize ourselves more clearly. Looking back, we can ask whether home—and
we ourselves—can do better.

From the Danube embankment, none of this looked like the edge of Europe. Trams
crossed the river; Parliament held its familiar place in the light; elections,
courts and offices carried the vocabulary of democracy. The acts inside my
story remained ordinary enough for each participant to continue. The question
was left between the beauty and the open route out: how long can a beloved home
ask a person to trade dignity for staying?

## 18. The Breaking Point Is Not the Beginning

The file can be made to begin with one charged line because that line is short.
It is also the version that makes everything before it disappear.

The public meaning begins earlier.

Anyone can delegate a task and remain responsible for it. Anyone can miss a
notice routed to a representative. Anyone can encounter a law that changes
faster than their professional support can adapt. Anyone can be physically
vulnerable when an institution expects ordinary performance. Anyone can have
their worst response become more legible than the path that produced it.

One line is easier to print than years of exclusion, lawful effort, repair and
endurance. Once that happens, cause and effect can change places. Pressure
becomes personality. The attempt to recover missing context becomes evidence
of obsession. Survival outside ordinary work becomes another condition to
explain.

That reversal is what makes the case public in meaning even while it remains
mine in fact. The danger is not that everyone would write my sentence. The
danger is that a low-trust system can first exclude a capable person, then use
his breaking point to explain away the system that excluded him.

The system was not an independent creature hovering above the country. People
made it each morning. A legislator accepted speed. A professional protected a
professional habit. An office defended the edge of its competence. A lawyer
ran out of time. An expert preferred a total explanation. A judge narrowed the
next question. Each choice could look ordinary from inside one role. Placed in
sequence, the ordinary choices became the machine.

That does not make every participant cruel, or every Hungarian participant the
same. It removes the comforting fiction that a broken system exists without
human maintenance. No single person designed the entire corridor.
Responsibility moved away from every desk; accountability moved toward the
person with nowhere further to send it.

# Part Six — A Private Case Enters Public Time

## 19. The Streets Begin to Speak

I did not join the KATA protest in 2022. Other citizens were already teaching
the city a different language: teachers, students and parents formed chains;
the presidential-pardon scandal filled public spaces; judges and court
employees later rallied for judicial independence and the freedom to speak.

By then emergency had become an ordinary political grammar. During COVID-19, a
curfew decided when a person could cross his own street. As the pandemic order
receded, the war next door supplied another state of danger. In 2025, the
capital's mayor announced Pride as a city event after the police had prohibited
the march. He was questioned, then charged. After the election and a European
court judgment, the charge was dropped. The paving stones had not moved. The
law around the people on them had.

These movements did not share one programme or know my case. Their cumulative
effect was to make institutional operation a public experience.

My own path was quieter and stranger. I moved through electronic forms.

I asked the Hungarian office charged with protecting fundamental rights to
look at the police process, the failures of representation and the
password-sharing trap. Its answer mapped the limits of its own jurisdiction:
it could not review how police and prosecutors weighed the evidence, could not
intervene in a pending court process, and directed parts of the complaint back
toward the same legal routes already surrounding me. A later answer said that
further submissions repeating the issue could be placed in the archive without
another reply. An office named for fundamental rights had given me a careful
map of the doors it could not open.

A petition to the European Parliament was declared admissible in January 2025.
The European Commission response described the data-protection framework and
pointed enforcement back toward national authorities and courts. The private
file had entered a legitimate public channel, only to be routed back toward the
same national architecture.

I had not become a politician.

I had become impossible to keep entirely private.

## 20. The Programme Hidden Inside the Complaint

The clemency request contained the sentence toward which all the other pages
had been moving:

> The strength of a state governed by law is revealed in the moments when it
> can recognize that an individual's failure is, in fact, a reflection of a
> system failure.

It did not say that the individual disappears into the system. My sentence
remained mine. It said that responsibility becomes dishonest when the last
visible error is detached from the machinery that made it likely and then used
to absolve that machinery from examination.

Over time, my proposals acquired a structure:

- notify the person directly when a representative acts;
- replace password sharing with granular authorization and acceptance;
- make every filing, rejection and correction visible as one event chain;
- record important outbound calls;
- create a health-crisis pause without erasing obligations;
- simplify tax categories around invoice data where possible;
- let the administration prepare a proposed return rather than force every
  small worker to reconstruct data the administration already holds;
- strengthen worker protection so that a nominal enterprise route is not the
  price of employment;
- introduce new tax systems in parallel, test them, then retire the old path;
- use AI to categorize and explain, not to hide responsibility behind another
  opaque decision.

Later, I remembered a public sentence about KATA-contracted software teams,
even inside the tax authority. For a moment, the state and the people it had
placed under suspicion appeared to have been buying labour through the same
door. Then the door was closed from inside, and the people outside were asked
to account for having used it.

The important change was personal. I had moved from demanding that somebody
repair my account to asking how the next person could avoid entering the account
in the first place.

That is political in the broad sense, but it did not begin with a party.

## 21. Four Years Later

On 12 April 2026, Hungary voted for a change of government.

The new majority could alter institutions that had appeared fixed. It promised
rule of law, judicial independence, anti-corruption and restored public trust;
critics warned that rapid constitutional change could reproduce the weakness it
claimed to repair. Trust cannot be restored merely by changing who controls the
speed.

In July, the former governing party marched under the words *Stop Autocracy*
and accused the new majority of dismantling constitutional democracy. The
warning might still be right. The same words had crossed the chamber.

The election was not a verdict in my case. It did show that continuity no
longer commanded enough trust. Institutions presented as fixed were now being
judged by the public beneath them.

One sentence from my 2022 messages acquired an uncanny echo. Its violent
language about twenty years and the rule of law had looked detached from
ordinary political possibility. Four years later, public actors were openly
describing a change of regime and restoration of the rule of law.

It was not prophecy. It was irony powerful enough to make time feel badly
edited.

## 22. Two Letters to Venice

The constitutional conflict eventually reached the Venice Commission.

The President of the Republic asked the Commission to assess the
attempt to end his mandate. His questions were dated 29 May and supplemented on
4 June 2026. He argued from the position of the highest office at risk.

At 14:18 on 5 June, I sent my own message to the same institution. Seventy-eight
minutes later, I forwarded the same letter to the Sándor Palace and the Prime
Minister's press office.

It was one small push from below.

The President defended the constitutional security of his office. I asked what
constitutional insecurity felt like beneath such offices: tax administration,
police procedure, forensic interpretation, clemency and years of uncertainty.

In July, a Venice Commission delegation met the country's senior leadership.
Public reporting showed consultation and caution, not a final opinion before
Parliament acted. The institution had received warnings from above and below
while Hungary's constitutional order stood visibly between states.

## 23. When an Eighth Judge Steps Aside

Venice was not the President's only route.

On 11 June, he turned to the Constitutional Court for an interpretation of the
limits around constitutional amendment and removal. The request returned him
to the institution he had joined as a judge in 2014 and led from 2016 until he
became President in 2024. The Court had fifteen members. Seven initially
declared that they were personally and directly affected and excluded
themselves from the matter. After that was communicated, an eighth judge also
declared exclusion.

The full sitting needed ten.

With only seven available, the Court could not form the quorum required to hear
the matter, and it disappeared from the agenda before a substantive decision.

The judges had not left office; a majority had stepped out of one case. Yet the
body asked to resolve a constitutional conflict could not assemble inside it.

This occurred while my own writings were trying to reach constitutional review
from below.

At one scale stood a President and a Court unable to assemble. At the other
stood one citizen, a laptop and an AI assistant translating lived chaos into a
legal route. Their authority was radically different; both images contained the
distance between a constitutional question and an answer.

The Constitutional Court had received my complaint through the trial court,
then stopped before the substance because the criminal case had not produced a
first-instance decision. The President's case lacked judges. Mine had reached
the constitutional door before ordinary procedure had finished with me.

Different cases had reached the same architectural fact: having a
constitutional question does not guarantee a room in which anyone will answer
it.

## 24. The Convoy and the List of Offices

After the election, the new Prime Minister publicly called for the departure of
officials appointed to long terms across the judiciary, prosecution and
oversight bodies. The detailed list belongs in the notes.

The list showed how power can survive an election through long appointments,
boards, rules, foundations and institutional habits.

One affair gave that transfer of power a physical image.

On 5 March, two armoured cash-in-transit vehicles travelling from Austria toward
Ukraine were stopped near Budapest carrying foreign currency and gold. The
scene made instant political cinema.

After the election, the shipment was returned, expulsions were withdrawn and
reviews began. A senior prosecutor resigned citing disagreement over the
investigation; tax-investigation leaders lost their appointments; the
Prosecutor General later resigned. The notes preserve the distinctions. What
reached my room was the speed of reversal.

The country could reopen a major enforcement operation, review agencies and
move senior offices in weeks. My own proceeding, built around one line and a
repaired tax return, had not reached a first judgment in years.

Speed did not make every replacement just. The institutions moved through
different legal hinges, and personnel change could still outrun repair.

The correct image is not a row of heads falling in one cut.

It is a wall of institutional portraits, each moving on a different hinge while
one person below them refreshes a screen and still does not know what will
happen at his next hearing.

## 25. The Empty Presidential Chair

On 18 July 2026, the President signed the constitutional amendment that ended his own
mandate on 20 July.

The parliamentary Speaker became interim President. As this edition closed on
8 August 2026, a new President had not yet been elected.

The empty chair was not a verdict in my case. It belonged to a country that had
decided continuity was no longer enough. It also carried a private irony.

I had already asked that office for mercy when mercy itself had become
politically dangerous. In February 2024, the disclosure of a presidential
pardon connected to the concealment of abuse in a children's home ended one
presidency and the public career of the former justice minister who had
countersigned it. The next President inherited a power that had burned the hands of
the people who used it before him.

I had never treated the person above that office as incidental. In November
2025, I came close to withdrawing my request because I did not consider its
occupant fit to decide it. I let the request continue.

The figures made caution almost absolute. In 2025, four of 689 decided
applications received clemency. In the first half of 2026, all 314 decisions
were refusals. Mine was dated 30 April.

On 19 May, while the same President still held office, I submitted revised
papers and asked that they be left with the incoming Justice Minister until the
presidency changed. Then I would ask again.

He had refused my clemency. The new majority refused him political mercy.

Before that refusal, the clemency process carried the same questions into my
home. A probation officer rang the bell without an appointment. The flat had
not been staged for inspection. It was puritan, untidy and unclean that day. I
own no car, usually do not heat the flat in winter, and spend little because
that is how I preserve independence. I can live that way. I was not asking the
visitor to pity me.

He asked what I lived on, why I was not working and what the apartment said
about my life. He called it a *bachelor flat*, referred to the proposed
suspended sentence and reacted with astonishment when he asked about the
disputed line. A life designed to need very little was once again being read as
a condition to be explained.

One constitutional task of the President is to express the unity of the
nation. While Parliament prepared to choose a new person for that chair, I was
moving in the opposite emotional direction. I had begun to consider whether
leaving Hungary for good might also mean renouncing my Hungarian citizenship.

I had returned from London because I wanted to live in my own country. The fact
that I could now imagine giving up the legal bond to it measured the distance
between national unity as an office and belonging as a lived experience. I had
not completed that act. The thought itself was the measure.

Exile usually means distance from home. I was living at home and felt exiled
from it.

Whether the replacement deserves trust depends on how it treats the same
problems when speed, power and inconvenience become its own.

# Part Seven — The System We Build Instead

## 26. The Product Returns

The real face of a system is not the front page of its portal or the sentence
in which it promises service. It appears when a person says: *the record is
wrong; the professional route failed; my body cannot meet your timetable; I
need somebody to carry the context from this room into the next one.*

By then, the state had begun to resemble the worst kind of relationship.

It had not been beside me while I searched for work, built independence abroad,
returned home, developed the product, secured the Belgian contract, repaired the
tax return or recovered alone from surgery. It appeared at the fracture. It
took the sentence, the devices, the fingerprints, the expert label and the
financial inventory. It claimed authority over the bad while contributing
almost nothing to the good. The relationship did not support. It extracted.

The danger did not require a secret conspiracy. It was already present in the
architecture: responsibility without shared information, delegated action
without safe identity, accusation without complete context, expertise without a
whole biography, delay without an ending and financial scrutiny before the
systemic cause had been answered. Built that way, a system could humiliate a
person, turn his own history against him, threaten the assets that allowed him
to survive and make departure feel safer than belonging. It could do this to
anyone unlucky enough to enter the same sequence.

For years, MyScoutee had asked how the natural first distrust between strangers
could soften without reducing either person to a verdict. It tried to create
enough private, reciprocal context for a real meeting to change the first
frame.

The legal story returned the question in a harsher form. The institution
classified first, and the whole person then had to fight his way back into the
record. Here the two lines met.

What should a system preserve before it classifies a person?

Direct notice. Visible actions. Consent that can be withdrawn. A record of who
did what. A route to correction. Private context rather than public humiliation.
Reciprocal evidence rather than one unanswerable score. Enough human space for
the next meeting to change the meaning of the previous one.

A healthy relationship is present in the good as well as the bad. A legitimate
system should meet the same minimum test. It cannot abandon the person during
work, illness and repair, then arrive only to punish the breaking point and
claim the life built without it.

MyScoutee cannot repair a tax authority, court or constitution. A social product
also has its own capacity for harm. Ratings can become gossip. Automation can
become coercion. Matching can hide a judgment inside a number. Local operators
can become unaccountable if the shared core provides no meaningful exit.

The lesson of Part II is therefore not that my product already contains the
answer.

It is that the product must survive the question.

## 27. Humour Is a Human Audit Trail

There is one quality no event log can fully capture.

In rooms where my future was being discussed, I sometimes made people smile.
The police uniform remained. The judge remained the judge. I remained the
defendant. Nothing procedural changed.

Yet the smile recorded that the roles were not the whole people.

Humour was not disrespect for the seriousness of the case. It was an informal
audit trail of humanity. To me, a shared smile briefly suggested that several
people could see the absurdity together.

The word was not added years later to make the story more literary. In one 2024
submission, I wrote that the frightening part was in the absurdity. In another,
I wrote that if everything had worked lawfully, I believed nothing would have
happened.

The humour also appeared in writing and images. I called myself “KATA's most
wanted,” turning the tax acronym and the accusation into the title of a farce.
In a Hulk-like image, a furious KATA figure held an axe in each hand while the
tax authority, prosecution and court hung from his belt. A Hungarian pun changed
*the reaper* into *the KATA taxpayer*.

Police recruitment posters supplied another impossible route. On 4 January
2024, I sent the police career office an English curriculum vitae (CV) and an
inquiry saying that I
had applied for the impossible position being administered at the station, had
reached the third round and was still waiting for feedback. I asked for the
earliest possible response. The accusation could progress through rounds while
the person inside it could not obtain an answer.

Later, asking German police to help the unresponsive Hungarian route extended
the same joke beyond the border. The humour was black because the need beneath
it was real. In a low-trust process, sarcasm became an unofficial receipt: a way
to record that a person had asked for contact when the official relationship
would not reliably acknowledge him.

The joke did not deny that some sentences were dangerous. It denied the solemn
fiction that the file's language was the only possible description of what had
happened. When officers struggled not to laugh, the laughter did not acquit me.
I experienced it as a brief admission that the institutional character and the
human being were not the same person.

In one police submission, I ended by declaring the institution fired. Elsewhere,
I sent institutions away to dispense justice in a pasture. A programmer with no
power to dismiss the state was dismissing the state in prose. Years later, an
election was followed by public demands for long-serving office-holders to
leave. The old joke acquired timing it could not have planned.

None of these lines transferred authority or predicted a legal result. Their
point was the impossibility. When every office could redirect me but I could
redirect none of them, sarcasm briefly reversed the arrows.

Humanity had not vanished from the rooms. It appeared in an embarrassed smile,
the second expert's different reaction, a lawyer asking for another room, a
judge's changing expression and even my inconvenient attraction while my
fingerprints were taken. The cruelty lay partly in the failure to carry those
moments forward. Every person could remain human locally while the combined
process behaved as though nobody were.

Normal conditions might have prevented the error chain. A measured transition,
clear authority, direct notice, one coherent record and a body given time to
recover could have left no criminal story to tell.

Instead, the system assembled a tragedy with comic timing.

## 28. The Invitation Changes

Part I ended by asking people to test MyScoutee in real communities.

Part II ends with a harder invitation.

Test the systems between us before pressure tests them for us.

The test is not whether a system can find an individual error. Every system can
do that. The test is whether it can still recognize itself in the error after
the individual has been found.

Do not wait for the frightened taxpayer, isolated worker, exhausted accountant,
overloaded official, defendant or judge to reveal every missing connection at
the worst possible moment.

Design the notification. Show the delegated act. Preserve the correction. Keep
the human being larger than the score. Let people leave a network without
leaving their dignity inside it.

I began this period as a programmer trying to remain legal and finish a foreign
contract.

I ended it—if this can yet be called an ending—sending institutional designs to
a government I had not helped elect as an activist, during a constitutional
transition I could not have planned.

Between those two moments, the proceeding moved from words on a screen to the
material remains of Part I: the apartment my parents had helped provide, the
belongings inside it, and savings built principally through work abroad. I had
returned because Budapest costs made the independence earned in London usable.
Now I felt that the price of trying foreign remote work from home might reach
back and consume the independence that had made the attempt possible.

The next hearing was scheduled for 16 September 2026. The new justice
administration had replied only that my submission was being forwarded for
handling. The country had an interim President. The final legal result did not
exist.

An older Hungarian instruction remained available: *strive, and trust*. I no
longer heard it as passive faith in authority. I heard it as the right to try
again, to look back from another world, and to ask whether home—and I—could do
better.

The line remains open.

### The government account and the later two-factor change

The “government account” in the narrative was **Ügyfélkapu**, Hungary's Citizen
Portal identification service. In 2022, the relevant login was based on a
username and password. From 16 January 2025, access to the same public-service
surfaces required either the two-factor Ügyfélkapu+ method or the Digital
Citizenship application. This later security change does not rewrite the 2022
scene.

Official guidance for accountants describes using another person's Citizen
Portal identity instead of acting as an authorized representative as a bad,
legally problematic practice. It points to registered authorization and the
Hungarian tax administration's (NAV's)
representation form as the lawful alternative. That is an identity boundary;
it does not, by itself, resolve the separate visibility problem in the
Founder's record: who received each submission result and notice, and whether
both sides could see the same chain.

Ügyfélkapu+ installs a secret into an authenticator through a setup
quick-response (QR) code and
then generates time-limited login codes from that secret. It follows
technically that copying the setup QR to another device can duplicate the
second factor. That inference explains why photographing a setup code may be
used as a workaround, but it is neither secure delegation nor an officially
endorsed accounting practice. Two-factor authentication protects a login; it
does not turn shared identity into granular authorization.

## Formal rules and lived practice

The memoir uses *low trust* to describe the repeated gap between what the rule
said, what people informally did to make work possible and who carried the risk
when the two routes separated. It is not offered as a diagnosis of Hungarian
character. Scholarship on Hungary and other post-socialist societies provides
the background distinction: formal institutions cannot be understood without
the practices, narratives and informal rules through which people actually use
them. Other research cautions against treating informal adaptation as evidence
of moral inferiority; it can be a response to organizational hierarchy,
