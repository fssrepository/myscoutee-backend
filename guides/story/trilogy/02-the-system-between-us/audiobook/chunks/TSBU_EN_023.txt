I had not become a politician.

I had become impossible to keep entirely private.

20. The Programme Hidden Inside the Complaint.

The clemency request contained the sentence toward which all the other pages had been moving:

The strength of a state governed by law is revealed in the moments when it can recognize that an individual's failure is, in fact, a reflection of a system failure.

It did not say that the individual disappears into the system. My sentence remained mine. It said that responsibility becomes dishonest when the last visible error is detached from the machinery that made it likely and then used to absolve that machinery from examination.

Over time, my proposals acquired a structure:

notify the person directly when a representative acts;

replace password sharing with granular authorization and acceptance;

make every filing, rejection and correction visible as one event chain;

record important outbound calls;

create a health-crisis pause without erasing obligations;

simplify tax categories around invoice data where possible;

let the administration prepare a proposed return rather than force every small worker to reconstruct data the administration already holds;

strengthen worker protection so that a nominal enterprise route is not the price of employment;

introduce new tax systems in parallel, test them, then retire the old path;

use AI to categorize and explain, not to hide responsibility behind another opaque decision.

Later, I remembered a public sentence about KATA-contracted software teams, even inside the tax authority. For a moment, the state and the people it had placed under suspicion appeared to have been buying labour through the same door. Then the door was closed from inside, and the people outside were asked to account for having used it.

The important change was personal. I had moved from demanding that somebody repair my account to asking how the next person could avoid entering the account in the first place.

That is political in the broad sense, but it did not begin with a party.

21. Four Years Later.

On 12 April 2026, Hungary voted for a change of government.

The new majority could alter institutions that had appeared fixed. It promised rule of law, judicial independence, anti-corruption and restored public trust; critics warned that rapid constitutional change could reproduce the weakness it claimed to repair. Trust cannot be restored merely by changing who controls the speed.

In July, the former governing party marched under the words Stop Autocracy and accused the new majority of dismantling constitutional democracy. The warning might still be right. The same words had crossed the chamber.

The election was not a verdict in my case. It did show that continuity no longer commanded enough trust. Institutions presented as fixed were now being judged by the public beneath them.

In July, the Council of the European Union approved a €10 billion recovery plan for Hungary. Access to the money was publicly tied to rule-of-law and anti-corruption reforms.

The number was the same. Its direction had reversed.

In 2023, a man who could not walk had written it while asking the state to account for twenty years. By July 2026 it stood in national policy beside the same words: rule of law.

22. Two Letters to Venice.

The constitutional conflict eventually reached the Venice Commission.

The President of the Republic asked the Commission to assess the attempt to end his mandate. His questions were dated 29 May and supplemented on 4 June 2026. He argued from the position of the highest office at risk.

At 14:18 on 5 June, I sent my own submission to the same institution. Seventy-eight minutes later, I forwarded the same letter to the Presidential Palace and the Prime Minister's press office.

It was one small push from below.

The President defended the constitutional security of his office. I asked what constitutional insecurity felt like beneath such offices: tax administration, police procedure, forensic interpretation, clemency and years of uncertainty.

In July, a Venice Commission delegation met the country's senior leadership. Public reporting showed consultation and caution, not a final opinion before Parliament acted. The institution had received warnings from above and below while Hungary's constitutional order stood visibly between states.

23. When an Eighth Judge Steps Aside.

Venice was not the President's only route.

On 11 June, he turned to the Constitutional Court for an interpretation of the limits around constitutional amendment and removal. The request returned him to the institution he had joined as a judge in 2014 and led from 2016 until he became President in 2024. The Court had fifteen members. Seven initially declared that they were personally and directly affected and excluded themselves from the matter. After that was communicated, an eighth judge also declared exclusion.
