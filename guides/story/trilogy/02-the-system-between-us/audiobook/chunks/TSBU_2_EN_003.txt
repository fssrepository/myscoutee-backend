This is not the story of an ordinary tax offence. Nor is it a legal brief with illustrations. It is the story of how the first biography was compressed until the second could fit the file—and how a period in the history of a country can be seen through that act of compression.

The compression began with a line on a screen. At the end of Part I, that line was a network connecting strangers. Here it became the ruled line of a form: a number, a date, a legal category, a signature. A home became an office. The office became a tax desk. The desk became a police scene. Eight days after the search, the police scene became a forensic examination. The resulting report was signed weeks later, but the transformation had already begun.

I had never been a party activist. When KATA (itemised tax for small businesses) changed in July 2022, others went to the bridges. I stayed at my computer and tried to protect a Belgian handover. I did not set out to confront a state. I tried to finish a piece of work, close a tax form and prepare for an operation.

That ordinary sequence matters. Kafka does not begin when a frightened man writes an extraordinary sentence. Kafka begins when everybody is performing an ordinary duty and the duties no longer meet: a government changes a route, a client still needs delivery, an accountant submits, a portal rejects, an official redirects, a taxpayer repairs, and a later institution reads only the sentence produced when the disconnected pieces fell onto one person.

That is where the irony begins.

Part One — The Legal Door.

1. The Year That Opened.

The man who returned from London did not return because London had defeated him. He returned because the arithmetic had finally changed.

Five years of shared rooms, restrained spending and foreign work had produced financial independence at Budapest prices. It was not wealth in London. In the flat my parents had helped provide, it was time: time to recover, travel, search for a partner and attempt the product that employment had never left enough space to build. Living with little had become a discipline. I believed that if I could remain independent of the next salary, I could endure almost anything.

By early 2019, I was searching for work again. Roughly fifty interviews failed. By August, I was close to returning to London when a multinational company hired me in Hungary almost by accident. Getting there contained its own small comedy. A local outsourcing company had first offered to place me at the multinational only if I became a sole proprietor and invoiced through KATA. I rejected the whole entrepreneurial arrangement, not merely that tax category. I wanted a salaried job, not to be turned into a business for somebody else's convenience. The smaller company judged my interview unsuccessful and wrote me off professionally.

Two weeks later, the multinational invited me into its own salaried process. The interviewers had not compared notes. When the earlier rejection surfaced in the second round, the room hesitated; then the company hired me directly, as an employee, for roughly twice the money. One accidental gap between institutions opened the stable door I had been trying to find.

KATA contractors at the same workplace still cost less than employees. Once I was inside, the apparently stable foothold became strangely hollow. I had very little work. I suspected that the smaller company's bad assessment had followed me as office gossip, but I never saw proof. A product manager repeatedly routed work to other teams. I spent long days in the office performing availability.

When I finally tired of waiting, I was placed alone between a German programme and a Hungarian organization. A single shared storefront was supposed to join ten national subsidiaries. The interface was singular; the marketing strategies were not. The Hungarian office's money came through its own marketing. On German calls, I presented a common shop whose countries had not agreed on one counter.

After almost a year, I finally said aloud that I had scarcely been given work. The sentence travelled—up toward German leadership, back toward Hungarian management, or both; I could not see the complete route. I entered the offices of several senior executives. Their questions made clear that the empty year was new to them. Then COVID-19 restructuring arrived, and my name was selected. I could not see whether one event had pushed the other. After months spent representing work that had nowhere to land, I was the part declared surplus.
