That dismissal felt like a tragedy. The ending itself was more humane than the year before it. I argued about the terms and negotiated a stronger severance payment than people around me expected. Their surprise briefly made the bargain feel like an achievement. It did not make the closed door smaller. I had spent years trying to enter stable employment; when I finally reached it, I lost the job before the job had properly found me.

COVID-19 made the next door difficult to find. One process at another multinational reached a fourth round. A former supervisor entered the conversation because he knew one of the leaders. He had not known the smaller-company prehistory and had not seen what the almost empty year looked like from my desk. I believed his intervention helped close the door, but I never saw the decision being made. No offer arrived.

The next salaried door opened at an investment bank in January 2021, while Hungary was under an evening curfew. In the office, faces had been reduced to eyes above masks. The bank required a long assessment and would not let me start until an outsourced background check returned. That check asked me to move identity documents through email and screening channels I considered old and weakly authenticated. The route took weeks and a large bundle of personal material. By the time it cleared, its awkwardness had become comedy. On my first day, I compared the handling of my data to Monty Python. The manager warned that this attitude could cost me the job. The screening had cleared. Trust had not.

At the end of the first week, compliance training revealed another gate. The bank's many employee-trading rules required approval around transactions in the retail Hungarian government bonds that held most of my savings. On one screen, the bond was household saving. On another, it was a controlled trade. These bonds were not an experiment for me. They were the seven-year floor beneath my independence.

The third week ended in a small masked theatre. The manager's anger was visible above the mask. Two solidly built colleagues sat smiling on either side of me while the termination papers landed on the table. A three-week employer stood on one side of the decision; seven years of security stood on the other. I chose the seven.

The previous dismissal had felt like a future being taken away. By the time I reached this room, I was already burned out. Nothing in the papers caught. On the way to the exit, I asked whether I could finally buy government bonds again. The dismissal passed through me. It did not matter.

An internal ethics review followed. For roughly three hours, I spoke with the bank's local lawyers. One question kept returning: had the manager threatened me? I said no. It was a cleaner answer than the memory. I had heard the warning on the first day and had chosen not to give it that name. I would not describe my answer as complete now. The institution received one word; I carried the rest of the scene out with me.

That masked room became my last Hungarian workplace. Almost six years later, it remains so. I did not know at the exit that I was leaving more than one building.

A year and a half separated that background check from the KATA change. The screening had been a gate I managed to pass. The later proceeding threatened to turn the lawful route back into work into the kind of record that could close such gates in the future.

During the next search, I first refused a Brussels project connected to a European institution. The contract placed too much risk on me, and I still believed I could find another salaried door. Eight months and dozens of interviews later, that belief had run out before my wish to work did.

The opportunity that finally held was a multinational telecom programme connected to Brussels. In ordinary times, it would probably have required me to leave Hungary again. During COVID-19, I did not dare fly into another relocation. Remote work made the geography temporarily negotiable: I could remain in my Budapest flat while working abroad. The contractual route passed through companies in more than one country. It looked less secure than the Hungarian job, but in practice it became the one workable professional path I had found.

The work gathered speed before I had arranged the life around it. I was already inside long Brussels calls, moving tasks from one person to the next, before I had an accountant. Between deliveries, I was still looking for the professional who would later handle the returns. The opening had arrived, and I stepped into it.

Stable work was never only income. Without it, partnership, a child and family life remained plans postponed until the ground stopped moving. By then, the years no longer felt renewable. The Brussels role arrived as the last opening through which work and the rest of life might still begin together.
