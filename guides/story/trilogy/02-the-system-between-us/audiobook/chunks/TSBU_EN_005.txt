I accepted it because independence was not meant to become permanent retreat. After years outside ordinary employment, I still wanted to work, contribute and belong to the country in which I lived. That decision matters later. The system did not reach me because I had avoided lawful work. It reached me immediately after I dared to enter it again through the only route that had opened.

It also required a legal form through which the work could be invoiced. Just over two years after I had rejected becoming an entrepreneur as the price of a domestic placement, I accepted it as the last available route into work. In September 2021, I became a sole proprietor using KATA.

I had no entrepreneurial revelation. I was not opening a café, selling paintings or building a personal brand. Informal foreign work might have left me outside the system's view. I chose the legal door instead. KATA was the bridge between a foreign job and the country in which I was still trying to live. The bridge existed because the pandemic had made another departure feel impossible.

That distinction matters. A tax form can look like a free choice after the fact while functioning as the price of entry at the time. Years of exclusion and unsuccessful job searches came first. Then one Hungarian employer happened to give me a foothold in 2019. COVID-19 broke it. Another attempt failed. The work I could finally secure was in Belgium and, without the pandemic, would have pulled me abroad again. I did not dare fly into another relocation during COVID-19, so I worked remotely from Budapest and used KATA as the legal bridge.

Every piece of that sequence had to fall in exactly that order. Remove the long exclusion, the chance Hungarian job, its pandemic collapse, the fear of flying, the Brussels contract or the temporary acceptance of cross-border remote work, and I do not enter KATA in 2021. The route did not arise from tax optimization. It was the narrow way back into work without abandoning my country again. When the state changed KATA at speed, it immediately closed that rare opening and used the obligations attached to it as the entrance into the rest of my life. Without the rushed transition, I could have moved into another tax form and continued working. Instead, after I corrected the return and paid the tax, the same sequence returned a year later as a criminal case.

I had wanted a way to work. The lawful route gave the system a way into everything else.

The work was real and demanding. Some days ran thirteen, fourteen or fifteen hours. Delivery schedules and production responsibility did not become lighter because I sat at home. A system elsewhere in Europe depended on tasks moving from one person to the next without an empty space between them.

The least stable-looking job gave me the most stable human place I had found in years. Many conference calls carried voices without faces. Through an open microphone in India came the putter of a tuk-tuk, returning me for a moment to my first stay in the south. At nearly ten thousand kilometres, colleagues made room for me inside the team. We shared thoughts I did not share with my closest friend. We worked so long beside one another that distance began to feel less important than presence.

In that team, respect often arrived before proof. I had grown used to caution arriving first and trust, if it came at all, appearing later. Here the order sometimes reversed. Courtesy was offered on credit. It did not remove hierarchy. When I wrote to one colleague that the middle layer seemed determined to keep me down while the senior manager kept protecting my work, an Indian voice at the other end answered without surprise. India had a caste system, he said; he believed the same pressure reached him too. Warmth and hierarchy occupied the same call.

Distance also failed in darker ways. A colleague died after what we understood at the time as a bite or sting near an Indian Ocean shore. The invitation that crossed the network next was to his funeral. We had entered one another's lives as voices. Grief made the ten thousand kilometres physical again.

The intermediary company also carried another familiar feature of that project: organizational chaos. Yet the senior manager who had invited me onto the project continued to believe in me and repeatedly brought the middle layer back into line. The client could see how far I had moved the work. When departure became unavoidable, they asked me directly, almost pleadingly, to stay.

I had spent Part I trying to solve empty spaces between people. Now my job was to prevent an empty space inside infrastructure.
