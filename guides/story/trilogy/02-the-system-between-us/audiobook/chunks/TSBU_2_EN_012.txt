At the first questioning, a woman took the defence chair. None of us knew who she was, and no one introduced her. Only later did I understand how far the substitution had travelled: the substitute for the appointed lawyer named in the papers had sent a legal assistant in his own place. She advised me to remain silent and gave me the office number. I called. Nobody answered, and nobody there spoke with me. The office, meanwhile, appeared to believe that the assistant was carrying the case. I had been searched, questioned and separated from my devices without receiving the complete material from which I could understand the accusation.

Five days later, police rang my bell again. They personally delivered a summons for three days later. It was urgent for them. They did not say why I had to appear.

Eight days later, I walked through the city to the police station. The loose nail travelled inside my shoe, shifting under the toe at every step. Saint Nicholas Day was approaching; shop windows were beginning to show the little bundles of twigs reserved for children who had misbehaved. Halfway there, I almost laughed. I should have brought the police one of those bundles.

Then the next step put the nail under me again.

Two forensic experts questioned me. The state already had a room in which to interpret my mind. I still did not have a stable person helping me interpret the case.

By the time I gave my actual police statement roughly a month later, in December, I was already suspected on two counts. The tax authority's two internal departments had been treated separately.

The later police interviews took place in ordinary rooms, not cinematic interrogation chambers. An officer sat opposite me behind a desktop computer. A court-appointed lawyer sat beside me. Another person sometimes remained behind me and sharply redirected me to answer the question being asked. The voice came from outside my field of view.

At one point my counsel asked for time. We moved into another room to speak. That small change of room mattered: the procedure briefly made physical space for advice before carrying us back into the official account.

The process also had a second, more mechanical room. Police escorted me there, photographed me and took my fingerprints. The woman guiding my fingers onto the paper was attractive to me. It was an inconveniently ordinary perception at the exact moment I was being converted into an image, a print and an entry in a file. Her work remained professional; my mind remained human.

The formal defence kept changing shape. The legal assistant warned that she would be unavailable while preparing for her professional examination. Calls to the office went unanswered, and for almost three months nobody there spoke with me. On 19 February, the appointed lawyer asked to be released because the office was overloaded. The police accepted the request, and it was from the police that I learned he was no longer my lawyer. I then tried to reach him by phone. Only then did he return my call. He told me to let the police do their job. For the months after 19 February, I did not know whether another defender had been appointed—or whether I had one at all. I complained in writing. Nobody replied.

On 11 June 2024, I was summoned back to the police station. A lawyer I had never met appeared beside me. Only then did I learn that, on paper, he had been my new appointed defender since 30 May. Nobody had told me. I did not need to ask. The policewoman conducting the hearing was called Kata. In a calm, ordinary voice, she said that the original appointed defender had not had time, as though that were the most ordinary thing in the world. The new appointed defender was already sitting beside me. The hearing continued.

The officers carrying the file changed as well. I could not reach them directly. I called the central number and first had to find out which investigator was currently investigating me before I could ask what was happening in the investigation.

Around 12 November, I returned alone to collect the investigation material. No lawyer sat beside me. A policewoman looked towards the waiting area almost timidly, checking that I was there and that the handover could begin. She seemed to freeze. A male officer glanced towards my operated leg and smiled. By then I trusted the continuity of no later account, so I made an undisclosed audio recording of the encounter. The act was not a victory over the process. It measured the low-trust condition: I no longer believed that even an ordinary handover would necessarily survive in one shared version unless I preserved it myself.
