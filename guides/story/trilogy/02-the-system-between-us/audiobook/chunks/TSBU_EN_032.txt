Formal rules and lived practice.

The memoir uses low trust to describe the repeated gap between what the rule said, what people informally did to make work possible and who carried the risk when the two routes separated. It is not offered as a diagnosis of Hungarian character. Scholarship on Hungary and other post-socialist societies provides the background distinction: formal institutions cannot be understood without the practices, narratives and informal rules through which people actually use them. Other research cautions against treating informal adaptation as evidence of moral inferiority; it can be a response to organizational hierarchy, scarcity and weak institutional trust.

These sources inform the narrative architecture. They do not prove the facts of the private case or make every Hungarian institution equivalent.

Interpersonal trust, hierarchy and the remote team.

The audio-only team, tuk-tuk, private conversations, senior-manager support, colleague's caste remark, death and funeral invitation are first-person memories. They describe one working relationship, not Indian or Hungarian people as fixed national characters.

A 2022 Ipsos online survey supplies limited comparative context: 56 percent of its Indian sample and 19 percent of its Hungarian sample answered that most people could be trusted. Ipsos cautions that its Indian sample was more urban, educated and affluent than the general population. The figures do not prove why this particular team behaved as it did.

Peer-reviewed correspondence research has separately found caste-based discrimination in Indian private-sector callbacks. That evidence makes the colleague's remembered answer socially legible; it does not identify his caste, prove the motive of any manager in this project or turn the team into a case study of India.

Emergency powers and public reversals.

The book joins several public moments to show a political atmosphere, not one continuous legal regime. A COVID-19-era decree imposed a night curfew and broad limits on assembly. A later state of danger was justified through the war in neighbouring Ukraine. These were distinct legal phases; the narrative does not claim that one automatically caused the other.

In 2025, after police prohibited Budapest Pride, the capital's mayor announced it as a municipal event and was later questioned and charged. Reporting in June 2026 records that the prosecution dropped the charge after a Court of Justice of the European Union judgment. The book uses the sequence as a public reversal, not as evidence about the Founder's criminal case.

In July 2026, the former governing party organized a Stop Autocracy protest and accused the new majority of dismantling constitutional democracy. That was a political allegation, not a finding that the new government was or was not authoritarian. The narrative retains the image because language once used against the old order had moved to the other side of Parliament.
