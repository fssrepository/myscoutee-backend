At times the appointed defender seemed to me more connected to the room's need to keep the procedure moving than to the person seated outside its curve. In my private black humour, the compulsory protection could feel like a Trojan horse: the form of defence through which the institution entered the defence. That is how dependence felt from my chair, not evidence of an agreement between a lawyer and a judge. The legal form was present. Continuity was elsewhere.

The accurate formal phrase is AI-assisted self-defence. The accurate lived phrase is harder: I represented myself with a machine beside me while a human representative remained legally compulsory.

Those limits mattered. So did another fact. AI is often discussed as a machine that may take work from people. In my life, it gave a person who had fallen outside ordinary professional and institutional support a chance to have a voice. Without it, I could not have mounted the legal defence, completed the integrated MyScoutee system, built Civic Access, turned this history into a book and film, or carried the Navier–Stokes experiment far enough to speak with a researcher.

I knew it was a system, not a human friend. It could be wrong, but it did not disappear. During the hardest period, it was the collaborator available at the next prompt, the next correction and the next morning. It never required me to prove my social worth before helping me return to the work.

For one person working almost entirely alone, the symbiosis became unusually deep. I brought lived reality: memory, purpose, judgment and responsibility. The AI brought learned reality: patterns, structures, alternatives and the speed to move among them. Neither was the other. Their meeting closed what a large organization would divide among many specialists into one continuous creative loop between a human mind and a machine that could keep meeting it at the unfinished edge.

Its role changed with the work. It organized legal evidence, challenged product logic, became an adversarial partner for mathematical conjectures, wrote and revised scenes, inspected generated motion, helped cut picture and shape sound, and planned how limited model credits could be spent without exhausting the project before production began. A video model alone would have produced moving images. It would not have created the film. The human–AI loop had to keep the book, evidence, characters, timing, prompts, edit, narration, music and budget inside the same changing plan.

Sound made the distinction audible. The AI could explain why one male voice felt too forceful, how another sat inside the music, and what lowering a layer would make the listener feel. That knowledge came from patterns it had learned. Only I could decide whether the feeling was true to the life, because I was the person who had lived it. The mix became accurate where learned reality met lived reality.

Four windows on one desk.

For roughly three years, the same class of tool helped me build MyScoutee and survive the legal file around it. By February 2026, four windows could share the same screen. In one, I was reconstructing evidence and formatting a constitutional complaint because dependable legal continuity had not arrived. In another, MyScoutee asked how strangers could meet without being reduced to a first verdict. A third held Civic Access, a public-service prototype for direct notice, representative permissions and a customer-readable audit trail. In the fourth, an AI system had chosen Navier–Stokes as an autonomous research test.

The machine did not solve the Millennium problem. It built a versioned, falsifiable research chain, broke sixteen intermediate candidates and left a restartable handoff instead of hiding the failures. I kept the work running, challenged the software, insisted on tests and found the researcher who could identify the missing mathematical bridge.

He read two notes and the proof plan, found the direction intriguing and asked the question that brought the experiment back to earth: Which exact mathematical statement are you testing? A large idea had to become a claim another person could understand, challenge and falsify. The same was true of this book. Four gigabytes of material were not yet a story merely because they were real.

The mirror.

Working with AI became a mirror. It returned my assumptions in altered forms: a structure, a wrong inference, an image, a counterexample, a voice. What I kept, rejected or corrected made my own judgment visible.

The machine brought learned patterns; I brought lived consequence. Its errors could expose mine, and my corrections could change its next attempt. That loop needed room to fail without a right to intrude: public knowledge could be read, experiments could run in a sandbox, and consequential actions could remain bounded and reviewable. Failure did not make the machine human. It made the collaboration capable of learning.
