Hundreds of thousands of Hungarians did not leave the country for one reason. They left for wages, housing, work, love, language, adventure and dignity in different proportions. This book follows one less visible pressure among them: the exhaustion of living where informal adaptation is required to make formal institutions work, yet the individual alone can be punished when that adaptation fails.

Prologue — The Other Biography.

Part I required a book to describe one person.

He came from agricultural ground and studied economics, programming and mathematics. He learned English through work in southern India, worked across continents, lived for five years in London and organized journeys through nearly fifty countries. Those journeys depended on strangers: a driver, a passenger, a hotel worker, a colleague, a couple sharing a taxi, somebody who noticed that the road ahead was unsafe. He saved through a shared bedroom and lunches he pretended not to need, then returned to Budapest with enough independence to build software intended to help people trust one another long enough to meet.

Part I was built from those meetings because human relationships cannot be cancelled out of the equation of a life. The driver, roommate, colleague, stranger, date and family member were not decorative anecdotes around the “real” biography. They were the forces through which decisions became possible, fear became bearable and a place became home.

Part I asked what a first judgment can erase between two people. Part II follows the same act after it acquires institutional force. A profile can end a conversation before a meeting becomes real. A label can begin to rewrite a life. In both cases, the shock lands inside the person being reduced, and what follows may be far larger than the thin first frame.

MyScoutee began with the gentler version of that problem. It tried to loosen the natural first distrust between strangers just enough for shared context to form before judgment became final. The system in Part II repeatedly lacked that capacity to receive the person it was classifying.

A forensic report later created another biography in a few paragraphs.

It described a man living through a fixed “global conspiracy,” incapable of positive feeling in human relationships, inclined to treat helpfulness as proof of bad intent, and driven by inferiority, projection and severe paranoid personality disorder. The report said this condition reached the level of pathological mental illness and moderately limited his capacity to recognize the consequences of his actions.

The same report recorded that he was calm and cooperative, understood the examination, observed ordinary forms of courtesy, was oriented in every respect and had intact comprehension, memory and intellect appropriate to his education. It found no earlier psychiatric documentation, no perceptual disturbance and no formal disorder of thought.

Quietness had always made people expect compliance from me. I was not quick-tempered; years could pass without anger reaching my voice. When it finally did, the sudden force could silence a room precisely because nobody had seen it coming. The file received the rare voice without the long quiet around it.

Both biographies carried my name.

Writing them together required a third position. I had to step outside my own fear and treat myself as one participant in the chain: not the innocent centre of every event, not the clinical character in the report, but a person whose actions could be examined beside everybody else's. That distance did not come from having too few attachments. It was necessary because there were too many. Every room led back to parents, colleagues, housemates, strangers, clients, officials and the country I had tried to make home. From inside all those bonds, the story was almost impossible to write. From slightly outside myself, its structure became visible.

A passport cannot settle a psychiatric question. It can expose the thinness of a caricature. My journeys had worked precisely because I could recognize help, trust unfamiliar people provisionally, revise a plan and experience warmth across languages. My software existed because I still believed human connection could be designed for without being controlled.

The security concern recorded by the experts was equally concrete. I objected to a system in which a personal government-account password could become the practical substitute for safe delegation, exposing taxpayers and accountants to actions performed under the wrong identity. The report preserved that argument, called my account contradictory and later translated the wider pattern into a “global conspiracy.”
