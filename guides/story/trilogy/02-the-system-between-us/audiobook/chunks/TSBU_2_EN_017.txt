The mathematical division of fault had failed me. The chain was simpler. The legislature moved at extraordinary speed. The accountant did not complete the work and continued to contest the route after I had reconstructed it. The administration routed notices and corrections through structures I could not read as one story, then sent the taxpayer back toward the representative. I remained legally responsible, corrected the return and paid the tax. The investigation later lifted the sharpest fragments out of that chain and interpreted the surviving person through a criminal and psychiatric frame.

By the time the consequence stopped moving, its entire weight rested on the person who had already repaired the underlying failure.

The hearing I entered alone.

The first court threshold was a preparatory hearing. The court-appointed defender had not worked through the case with me before it. He asked me to bring my defence material on a pen drive and hand it over at the hearing. That request was the extent of our practical preparation.

At the last moment, he forwarded his own hearing invitation as it was. The clerk's email address remained visible on it. I did not yet know the court's electronic filing system; that accidental address gave me a route to send my two-page defence before the hearing. The facts, position and decisions in those pages were mine, while an AI assistant helped me shape them. I still entered the room without a human defender who had worked through the chain with me.

I had read the notice but did not yet understand the lived protocol. I could admit guilt as charged and give up the right to a trial, or contest the account and identify the facts and evidence on which my defence depended. Individual facts could be accepted, but the central choice could not be half-made. The prosecution proposed two years of imprisonment, suspended, if I admitted the charge. Two years was the statutory floor of the offence, not a sentence below its ordinary range. Suspension was the offer. The price was the prosecution's story becoming mine.

I contested it. A decision spoken in minutes opened a trial measured in further years.

I asked human-rights organizations for help. The replies pointed to limited capacity. After the preparatory hearing, I also consulted a private lawyer. I remember leaving with two things I had not possessed when I entered: a clearer understanding of how to read the architecture of the charging document, and a fee quotation of two million forints plus value-added tax (VAT) if the defence continued there. The consultation also turned quickly toward the psychiatric label, as though the label could explain the person before the life had been read.

The difference between us was contained in one word. For a lawyer, the label could be a familiar category in a file. For me, it entered the part of the Constitution that calls human dignity inviolable.

Inside the curve.

Approaching court, Budapest first remained visible as home—the Danube, the city and the life outside the case. Then the long brown institutional façade on Fo Street filled the frame. Its rhythm and mass felt almost Roman: monumental, closed and frightening. A human story was entering stone.

The hearing room had a practical geometry. A C-shaped desk faced the seats where I sat. From my position, the prosecutor was on the left, the clerk at the far-left corner, the judge in the centre and my court-appointed defence counsel on the right. I sat in front of them with a table microphone directly before me. It did not look theatrical. Its shape nevertheless made the roles—and my place outside their working curve—immediately visible.

The hearing also had a recurring expression: a smile that never became relief. When I said that I had been afraid, the judge answered with almost amused disbelief: You were afraid? When I explained the precautions and defensive routes I had invented while no stable defence carried the chain, she smiled: And you defended yourself. When I said that I had managed to work again for two and a half months, the response moved back toward the amount of the financial consequence.

The same collision appeared around the intimate history I had sent to the tax administration while awaiting surgery. I had tried to explain what Part I had cost: the shared London room, hunger disguised from colleagues, the years of saving and the reason I had dared to return. The judge asked whether I really had to write all that when nobody cared. Later in the hearing, when the money returned to the centre of the room, I answered before I could soften it: then it should interest them, because that was where the money had come from. The court wanted the origin of the savings with the life that had produced them removed.
