I don't remember my life as a straight line. A street becomes a room, a window becomes another country, a glance becomes a verdict before anyone has time to speak. Memory edits without asking permission. Sometimes the only thing that survives the cut is a smile.

I remember routes before dates: a card at a gate, a voice behind a bus seat, a taxi shared with strangers, luggage falling, hands lifting a balloon basket from a field. Global route. Local encounter. The world was large; the moments that stayed were human.

Work should have been simpler than travel. Three people, one problem, three screens. A form changed, a rule moved, a deadline arrived. Nobody had to be cruel. The gap between ordinary actions was enough. I kept trying to repair the record while the work kept moving.

Then the system entered the story without ever becoming one person. A voice at the door. A search. A lawyer I could not quite reach. An officer who almost laughed. People could stay human while the process forgot that they were. When the rooms stopped connecting, an AI helped me hold the whole story together — and sometimes simply keep going.

A file can hold a photograph, a fingerprint, a signature, a list of things you own. It cannot hold why you came home, what you gave up, or what you were trying to repair. I asked the system to make room for repair. It refused. Later, two very different lives reached for the same distant door — one from above, one from below.

But humanity kept leaking through. A stranger opened a gate. Someone smiled in the wrong room. People pulled together when a balloon landed where it should not. A judge, an officer, a lawyer, a dancer: for a second, each role became a person again. Whole institutions could move in weeks. My file could wait for years. Even the chairs above it changed. Then the system resumed.

By then the city was reading me too. London, Budapest, every memory stayed in the glass until the reflections disappeared. What remained was not an answer: a room, a body, another human face.
