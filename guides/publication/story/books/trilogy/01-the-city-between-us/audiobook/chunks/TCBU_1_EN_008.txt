On some visits home from London, I used online dating in Budapest while hiding that London was where I actually lived. I wanted to enter the immediate local possibility without letting the distance define me first. But the distance would have defined any continuation, and withholding it prevented the other person from judging the real plan. The concealment was mine.

During that period I also met a former colleague who, in my memory, had barely noticed me while I was living in Budapest. The new encounter became intense almost immediately. She spoke in the language of love and of wanting a child before there was enough shared life for me to understand what the declaration meant. I interpreted the suddenness as a wish for motherhood attaching itself to me, rather than love growing from knowledge of me. That was my interpretation, not access to her private motive. What I can say safely is that speed without shared context frightened me, while my own hidden London life made the context less complete from the other direction too.

I sometimes imagine how different those years might have felt if a fast, group-first local path had already existed. It could not have promised me a partner or removed the price of housing. It might have helped the city's extraordinary variety become recurring human context sooner: a small local group, a shared activity, another invitation, and people who no longer vanished when one event ended.

London could have been better still—not because its magic was false, but because more of that magic might have become belonging. This is a counterfactual hope, not a product result I can prove.

In December 2015, after almost five years, I came home.

Not because the first three years had been false, but because they had been real—and were no longer enough.

Part Three — Global Routes, Local Encounters.

9. The Fare Was One Pound.

Travel began in London as an argument with limitation.

I could not buy comfort, so I bought movement. Once, an overnight coach to Edinburgh cost one pound. The price turned sleep into an optional extra. I spent the night inside the shared atmosphere of strangers, including the unmistakable smell of somebody else's socks, and arrived in Scotland carrying fatigue as proof of admission.

I went because the adventure was larger than the inconvenience.

From Edinburgh, the country opened farther. Loch Ness replaced the contained geometry of London with water, weather and distance. On the River Clyde in Glasgow, a powerboat transformed the industrial waterfront into speed, spray and bridges. At a ticket counter, a German tourist considered the price for his child too high. I was short of money by London standards, yet for a moment the usual economic relation was reversed: the German tourist was the one who could not cover the child's ticket, and I, the Hungarian stranger beside him, almost paid it for him.

Travel revealed parts of me that ordinary life kept hidden. Thrift could live beside generosity. Fear could live beside curiosity. Exhaustion could become a story before it had even ended.

This is what I mean by a global route becoming a local encounter. The map takes you somewhere. A person, a voice, a price, a misunderstanding or an unexpected act gives the place human scale.

10. The Extra Day Nobody Had.

I once planned a road circuit from London through Manchester, Liverpool, Barmouth and Cardiff, then back to London. Five of us travelled: two couples and me. I cannot drive, so we rented a car and one of the acquaintances travelling with us drove it. He was part of the group, not a hired driver. I designed the route, the sequence and the timing. We used budget hotels along the way.

The plan worked. That was almost the problem.

The rented car followed the itinerary with impressive precision. In Manchester, brick, industry, libraries and new glass occupied the same streets. Liverpool opened toward the Mersey, the Pier Head and the old docks. At Barmouth on the North Wales coast, two lines of hills seemed to lead the eye toward the sea. At low tide we could walk far onto the exposed shore and look back at the town held against the slope. Cardiff added castle stone, arcades and the broad movement toward the bay.

Everything happened on time. Everything happened too quickly.

The route had three days. Nobody travelling with me could make a fourth day available, and that missing day compressed a beautiful circuit into an exercise in endurance. The car had five people and the plan reached every stop. What the journey did not have was slack.
