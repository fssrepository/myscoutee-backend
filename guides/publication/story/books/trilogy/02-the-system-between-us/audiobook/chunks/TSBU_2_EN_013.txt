The male officer's smile brought back the obscene visual joke I had used to show the tax administration that behind the case was a man recovering from surgery. Where was the grave threat in that image? It had been a huge symbolic kick aimed at an institution's backside. That was what came back to me as they finally handed over the papers I had been waiting for.

The sentence had time. The search had time. The experts had time eight days after the search. Defence was the part of the system that repeatedly ran out of it.

During that silence, I even contacted German police. I did not imagine that a foreign police service could take over a Hungarian criminal file. I was asking outsiders, in effect, to help their Hungarian counterparts establish a human route back to a person who could not obtain one at home. The request measured the isolation more accurately than any legal theory could.

At other moments, the laughter belonged to the institution. The investigator laughed in my face when I explained the password-sharing trap and said I had done something stupid. That was precisely the inversion. A routine workaround had required the unsafe sharing of identity; when the workaround failed, its absurdity was returned to me as personal foolishness.

I used humour.

Humour was how a human being remained present when every other available role was defendant, officer, device owner or subject. A police officer could ask a hard question, I could answer in a way that exposed the absurdity, and for a second somebody smiled.

Later, a judge could smile too. The smiles decided nothing. They showed only that the people in the room could still recognize the absurdity while the procedure continued to manufacture it.

The irony kept me from becoming only the file.

At first, the frightening thing was the name of the accusation. Later, the more frightening discovery was how little trust remained. An institution could still issue an order, seize a device or compel my attendance. But after referrals, missing answers and words repeatedly detached from their context, it could say almost anything and expect the sentence itself to carry authority. It no longer did. The words retained coercive force while losing human and moral weight. Power without credibility was more frightening than the label attached to me.

So I held up a mirror. If the institution could act as though it were entitled to anything, I could perform the same entitlement back at it. I did not possess the power I performed. The performance exposed the asymmetry: its words could still compel me; mine could only make the room briefly see what that felt like.

For a moment, nothing seemed to matter because the common rule between us had already disappeared.

12. Remarks in the Margin.

My later defence submissions preserve the institutional voices as short fragments. On the page, they look like remarks written in the margin of a life:

“He can use the library for the internet.”

“Why did he come home?”

“Then what does he live on?”

“The appointed lawyer didn't have time.”

The two expert chairs added their own fragments:

“Why did you need an accountant?”

“That will get you life, for sure.”

“An old man.”

“You weren't successful.”

“Why didn't you see a psychologist?”

Each sentence had belonged to a particular room and a practical purpose. Read together, they turned biography into an examination.

Why had I returned?

Part I required a whole book to answer that.

What did I live on?

The government-bond account did not begin in a courtroom. It began in a shared London bedroom and at Soho lunch tables where I went with my colleagues, watched them eat and pretended I was not hungry. It continued through years of foreign work, low-cost journeys and a Budapest flat that stood empty while I was away. My parents had helped provide the flat. The reserve came principally from the life I had lived abroad.

At Budapest prices, those savings bought time. Across the eleven years after I left London, I spent roughly nine outside conventional employment: searching, travelling, surviving on reserves and building work no employer had commissioned. Planning did not make exclusion painless. It stopped exclusion from becoming immediate collapse.

The court asked about the apartment. The apartment was not enough. The questions continued through the belongings inside it, the bond account, the savings and what else I owned beyond them. The objects in the questions were also compressed scenes: the empty flat, the shared room, the uneaten lunches, the work performed far from home. I had built security abroad so that I could return and live in my own country. Now the case was moving backwards through that security as a list of assets.

Why had I needed an accountant?
