24. The Convoy and the List of Offices.

After the election, the new Prime Minister publicly called for the departure of officials appointed to long terms across the judiciary, prosecution and oversight bodies. The detailed list belongs in the notes.

The list showed how power can survive an election through long appointments, boards, rules, foundations and institutional habits.

One affair gave that transfer of power a physical image.

On 5 March, two armoured cash-in-transit vehicles travelling from Austria toward Ukraine were stopped near Budapest carrying foreign currency and gold. The scene made instant political cinema.

After the election, the shipment was returned, expulsions were withdrawn and reviews began. A senior prosecutor resigned citing disagreement over the investigation; tax-investigation leaders lost their appointments; the Prosecutor General later resigned. The notes preserve the distinctions. What reached my room was the speed of reversal.

The country could reopen a major enforcement operation, review agencies and move senior offices in weeks. My own proceeding, built around one line and a repaired tax return, had not reached a first judgment in years.

Speed did not make every replacement just. The institutions moved through different legal hinges, and personnel change could still outrun repair.

The correct image is not a row of heads falling in one cut.

It is a wall of institutional portraits, each moving on a different hinge while one person below them refreshes a screen and still does not know what will happen at his next hearing.

25. The Empty Presidential Chair.

On 18 July 2026, the President signed the constitutional amendment that ended his own mandate on 20 July.

The parliamentary Speaker became interim President. As this edition closed on 8 August 2026, a new President had not yet been elected.

The empty chair was not a verdict in my case. It belonged to a country that had decided continuity was no longer enough. It also carried a private irony.

I had already asked that office for mercy when mercy itself had become politically dangerous. In February 2024, the disclosure of a presidential pardon connected to the concealment of abuse in a children's home ended one presidency and the public career of the former justice minister who had countersigned it. The next President inherited a power that had burned the hands of the people who used it before him.

I had never treated the person above that office as incidental. In November 2025, I came close to withdrawing my request because I did not consider its occupant fit to decide it. I let the request continue.

The figures made caution almost absolute. In 2025, four of 689 decided applications received clemency. In the first half of 2026, all 314 decisions were refusals. Mine was dated 30 April.

On 19 May, while the same President still held office, I submitted revised papers and asked that they be left with the incoming Justice Minister until the presidency changed. Then I would ask again.

He had refused my clemency. The new majority refused him political mercy.

Before that refusal, the clemency process carried the same questions into my home. A probation officer rang the bell without an appointment. The flat had not been staged for inspection. It was puritan, untidy and unclean that day. I own no car, usually do not heat the flat in winter, and spend little because that is how I preserve independence. I can live that way. I was not asking the visitor to pity me.
