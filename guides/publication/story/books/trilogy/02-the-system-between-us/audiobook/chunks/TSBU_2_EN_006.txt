2. A Distorted Choice Can Still Be Legal.

KATA was attractive because it was simple compared with the alternatives. That simplicity also distorted the market. Employers and intermediaries could look at a worker and see not only a person's labour but a tax route. The worker could be described as an entrepreneur while carrying the dependence of an employee without all the protection of employment.

The category contained very different lives, from genuine small businesses to workers carrying employee-like dependence through an enterprise form. The state had allowed those lives to accumulate inside it for a decade.

When a route becomes normal, people build obligations around it. Accountants develop habits. Software systems encode assumptions. Contracts are signed. Families calculate. Foreign clients schedule work. A government can still change the route. The manner and speed of change determine how much damage arrives before the new rule has even been understood.

On Monday 11 July 2022, the new KATA bill appeared. On Tuesday, Parliament passed it under an exceptional procedure. It was due to take effect on 1 September.

Hundreds of thousands of people who had organized work around the old route now had weeks to decide whether to close, change tax systems, renegotiate contracts or stop working through that form. Their accountants had the same weeks to translate the change for all of them at once.

For a few weeks, roughly 450,000 taxpayers entered the same narrow corridor: contracts, invoices, family plans, accountants and closing dates all moving at once. Most found another door. In a few lives, the pressure kept travelling long after the tax account should have closed. Mine was one of them.

People went into the streets. Bridges were blocked. The public argument became immediate: fairness, abuse, livelihood, consultation, legal certainty.

I was not there.

I was trying to understand what happened to a live contract, pending delivery, invoices, foreign income, a closing business and an accountant who also had to interpret the transition for many clients at once.

The law moved in a day. Understanding did not.

3. Protecting a Handover.

My contract was extended on 27 July. That did not mean the problem had been solved. It meant I was trying to gain enough time to leave without dropping work that other people depended on.

By 3 August, I was treating the change as a force-majeure exit. I arranged the handover and invoicing around the work that still had to move. I gave up the remaining value of later months rather than continue inside a route I no longer understood as safe.

The team did not release me lightly. The client asked me to remain; the senior manager who had brought me in still backed me. The relationship had become more durable than the legal route carrying it.

Nobody could move cleanly forward or back. The client still needed a proper handover, the accountant was interpreting the same sudden transition for many clients, and I was trying to close a live project without abandoning it. The handover could not be completed inside the remaining KATA period. I therefore had to issue an advance invoice before the cutoff for work whose transfer would continue beyond it.

From the outside, an invoice is a rectangle containing a date and amount. From inside the project, it was time: work already performed, work still being transferred, a foreign client's expectations, the accountant's interpretation and a new Hungarian rule whose consequences were still moving.

This is where the story could have ended.

A normal transition would have closed the business, reconciled the records, confirmed the final amount, delivered direct notices to the person legally responsible, and allowed everyone to move on.

Instead, the transition created a three-person chamber with no shared window.

Part Two — Three People, No Shared Screen.

4. The Low-Trust Triangle.

There were three principal participants in the tax sequence: my accountant, the tax administration, and me.

Each had a different view.

The accountant could submit forms and receive technical messages. The tax administration could process, reject, reclassify and call back. I remained legally responsible, but I could not automatically see every delegated action or every notice in the same form and at the same time as my representative.

The triangle should have been held together by shared information. Instead, each side saw a different record at a different time. The accountant submitted and received technical messages. The tax administration processed, rejected and later altered account entries. I carried the legal responsibility without seeing the same continuous chain.
